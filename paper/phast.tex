\begin{abstract}
We present \PHastR, a minimal perfect hash function of the \PHast family. Like
\PHastP, the fast-to-build variant of \PHast, \PHastR finds feasible seeds with
bit-parallel operations: its seeds move keys along \emph{rings} of slots, and
to evaluate the seeds of a bucket it cyclically shifts, for each key, a word
recording which slots of its ring are used. Unlike \PHastP, however, it avoids
\emph{self-collisions}, which make two keys of a bucket collide for (almost)
all its seeds: a third of the buckets bumped by \PHastP with wrapping
contain one. With 8-bit seeds, \PHastR uses $1.94$ bits per key, the same
space as the best variant of \PHastP with wrapping, but it is built in
$42\%$ of the time, and its queries are $28\%$ faster on $10^9$ keys, as it
bumps $0.5\%$ of the keys instead of $3.9\%$; they are faster also than those
of \PHast, which takes eleven times as long to build, and about as fast as
those of PtrHash, which uses more than one bit per key more. With a larger
expected bucket size
\PHastR comes within $0.02$ bits per key of the space of \PHast, using
$1.90$ bits per key, and still has faster queries. Its construction also scales
better with the number of threads: with all the threads of a 48-core machine
it is almost three times faster than that of \PHastP. Finally, we show that
when each key can be placed only within a range of $W$ slots, every
placement of $n$ random keys on $n$ slots, however found, must bump a
constant fraction of the keys, about $1/(2W)$ on large key sets: this floor
is about one tenth of the keys bumped by \PHastR.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A \emph{minimal perfect hash function} (MPHF) maps a static set of $n$ keys
bijectively to $[0\..n)$; it requires at least $\lg e\approx 1.443$
bits per key~\cite{FKSSSFPHF} (we use $\lg$ for the binary logarithm and $\ln$ for the natural
logarithm). Among the many practical constructions, \emph{bucket placement}
gives the fastest queries: keys are hashed to buckets, and a per-bucket seed
chooses for each key of the bucket a \emph{slot} of $[0\..n)$, so that no two
keys collide.
The idea goes back to FCH~\cite{FCHFACMPHF}, which, however, was slow to
build unless its functions used a lot of space, and to Pagh's
hash-and-displace~\cite{PagHDEEMPHF}; it was brought back by
CHD~\cite{BBDHDC}, and then by PTHash~\cite{PTPRFMPH}, followed by many
others, such as PHOBIC~\cite{HLPPPHOBSIC} and PtrHash~\cite{RagPTR}.

\PHast~\cite{BeSPHast} pushes this approach to its limit: the seeds are
stored in a plain array of fixed-width values, each seed places the keys of
its bucket inside small overlapping slices of the output range, and a query
reads a single seed for almost all keys. Finding the seeds, however, is
expensive, as \PHast tries all the seeds of each bucket: construction takes
hundreds of nanoseconds per key. Its variant \PHastP finds feasible seeds
with bit-parallel operations, and it is an order of magnitude faster to
build, but with 8-bit seeds it needs $2.11$ bits per key instead of $1.88$.
Its variants \emph{with wrapping}, in which keys wrap around the end of their
slices, need less space ($1.94$ bits per key for the best one, that is, the
one using the least space), but they are slower to build.

In this paper we present \PHastR, a structure of the same family that, with
8-bit seeds, has almost the space of \PHast, faster queries, and, with
several threads, a construction faster than that of \PHastP.

The idea behind \PHastR lies between \PHast and \PHastP. In \PHastP a seed
moves all the keys of a bucket by the same amount: this makes it possible to
evaluate many seeds at once with bit-parallel operations, but two keys of a
bucket that collide for one seed collide for (almost) all seeds. In \PHast,
instead, each seed places the keys independently, but seeds must be tried
one at a time. In \PHastR the lowest bits of a seed choose one of a few
independent \emph{layouts}, each assigning an offset to every key, and the
remaining bits choose a \emph{rotation}, which moves all the keys of the
bucket by the same amount along their \emph{rings} of slots: all the rotations of a layout can be evaluated at
once, as in \PHastP, but two keys collide for (almost) all seeds only if they
do so in every layout, which is very unlikely. Since bumping is where both
the excess space and the slow queries come from, \PHastR gets almost the
space of \PHast; and since the two shifts needed to choose a layout are hidden by the
access to the seed, its queries are even faster.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \PHastR and its bit-parallel search, which finds all the rotations of a
layout that map no key to a used slot by cyclically shifting, for each key,
the bits recording which slots of its ring are used
(Section~\ref{sec:phastr});
\item an analysis of the differences from \PHast and \PHastP, showing that
the excess space of \PHastP, with and without wrapping, comes from bumping,
that a large part of the bumping is caused by self-collisions inherent to
the additive scheme of \PHastP, and that layouts make their effect
negligible
(Section~\ref{sec:differences});
\item an implementation in the Rust crate \texttt{sux}, an experimental
comparison with the reference implementation of \PHast and \PHastP and with
PtrHash (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), and an offline construction that reads the
keys just once, needs on large key sets less than half a byte per key of
memory, and builds the same
structure as the construction in memory (Section~\ref{sec:offline});
\item an analysis of the number of keys that every placement must bump when
each key can be placed only within a range of $W$ slots: on large key sets
it is about a fraction $1/(2W)$ of the keys, so no search can produce a first
level free of bumping, but \PHastR bumps about ten times as many keys as
necessary (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We refer the reader to the survey by Lehmann et
al.~\cite{LMPPSVWMMPHS} for a complete account of the field, and recall here
the approaches that are relevant to this paper. MPHFs based on
\emph{fingerprinting}, such as BBHash~\cite{LRCPFSMPHMKS} and
FMPH~\cite{BelFBMPHR}, map the keys to bit arrays of decreasing size, one
level at a time: keys that collides in one array are \emph{bumped} to the next array; 
they are fast to build, but they use more than two bits per
key. At the other end, MPHFs based on \emph{recursive splitting}, such as
RecSplit~\cite{EGVRMPHRS} and its parallel implementation~\cite{BKLSHPCRBMPHF},
ShockHash~\cite{LSWSTOSMPHBBF}, and CONSENSUS-RecSplit~\cite{LSWCSESAMPH}, get
close to the lower bound of $\lg e$ bits per key, at the price of slower
queries; SicHash~\cite{LSWSSICTPH} combines small irregular cuckoo hash
tables with retrieval data structures.

\emph{Bucket placement} goes back to FCH~\cite{FCHFACMPHF} and to Pagh's
hash-and-displace~\cite{PagHDEEMPHF}, in which each bucket stores a displacement
that moves all its keys by the same amount: two keys of a bucket that collide
for one displacement thus collide for all of them, and the scheme requires the
pair of hash functions locating the keys to be injective on the key set.
CHD~\cite{BBDHDC} brought the idea back: the seed of a bucket selects a function
from a family of random functions (in practice, linear combinations of two hash
functions of the key with coefficients depending on the seed), so that different
seeds place the keys of a bucket independently; seeds are tried one at a time,
in order, and then compressed. PTHash~\cite{PTPRFMPH} revisited FCH with a
careful engineering of the encoding of the seeds (its \emph{pilots}) and of the
distribution of the keys into buckets, and it was followed by
PHOBIC~\cite{HLPPPHOBSIC}, which optimizes the bucket sizes and interleaves the
encoding of the pilots, and by PtrHash~\cite{RagPTR}, which stores the pilots in
bytes and replaces the greedy placement of the buckets with a cuckoo-like search
that evicts already placed buckets, obtaining very fast queries. Finally,
\PHast~\cite{BeSPHast} combines fixed-width seeds and slices with bumping: buckets
that cannot be placed are left to the following levels, so that the search never
backtracks.

\PHastR inherits the structure of \PHast, and its seeds lie between the
additive displacements of hash-and-displace and \PHastP and the independent
functions of CHD and \PHast: like the linear combinations used by CHD, a seed
both changes the relative positions of the keys of a bucket (by choosing a
layout) and moves them all by the same amount (by choosing a rotation), but
the few layouts are obtained for free from the computation of the bucket,
and all the rotations of a layout are evaluated at once with bit-parallel
operations.

\section{\PHastR}
\label{sec:phastr}

\PHastR follows the scheme of \PHast~\cite{BeSPHast}; we discuss the
differences in Section~\ref{sec:differences}. Each key has a $64$-bit hash,
which determines its \emph{bucket}, among $B$ buckets, and its \emph{slice},
an interval of $L$ consecutive slots of the \emph{output range} $[0\..m)$
whose start grows linearly with the hash. Each bucket has a seed, stored in a
plain array of $S$-bit values; the seed maps each key of the bucket to a slot
of its slice. A slot is \emph{used} if a key has already been mapped to it, and
\emph{free} otherwise; a seed is \emph{feasible} for a bucket if it maps its
keys to distinct free slots. The structure is a sequence of \emph{levels}
using the same scheme: buckets for which no seed is feasible are
\emph{bumped} (seed~$0$), and their keys are handled by the following
levels. The first level has $m=n$, so every bumped key leaves a
free slot (a \emph{hole}) in the first level, and an Elias--Fano
sequence~\cite{EliESRCASF,FanNBRISM}, the \emph{remapping}, maps the slots of
the following levels, in order, to the holes. Since bumped keys are few, a query reads a
single seed for almost all keys. Seeds are assigned by a \emph{sweep} over
the buckets: buckets enter, in order of index, a priority queue that favors
large buckets, the \emph{window}, which holds the buckets not yet placed
among the $256$ starting from the first bucket not yet placed; the sweep
repeatedly extracts the bucket of highest priority and gives it the feasible
seed minimizing the product of the distances of the slots of its keys from
a point slightly before the slices of the bucket.

Each hole costs about $2+\lg(1/\beta)$ bits in the remapping,
where $\beta$ is the fraction of bumped keys, and the bumped keys must be
placed again by the following levels, which in turn bump a fraction $\beta$
of their keys, so that overall the levels place about
$n(1+\beta+\beta^2+\cdots)=n/(1-\beta)$ keys. With $S$-bit seeds and expected
bucket size $\lambda=n/B$ the seeds take $S/\lambda$ bits for each key placed,
and the space in bits per key is thus approximately
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:space}
\frac{S}{\lambda(1-\beta)} + \beta\Bigl(2+\lg\frac1\beta\Bigr),
\end{equation}
and since the entropy of a random set of $\beta n$ holes is already about
$\beta n(\lg(1/\beta)+\lg e)$, the encoding of holes cannot be
substantially improved: once $S$ and $\lambda$ are fixed, \emph{the only
lever is $\beta$}. We will show in Section~\ref{sec:limits} that on large key
sets no placement, however found, can avoid bumping altogether; the purpose of
the design of \PHastR is to bump few keys while finding feasible seeds with
bit-parallel operations.

\paragraph{Placement scheme.}
We denote with $\hi(x)$ and $\operatorname{lo}(x)$ the upper and lower $64$
bits of a $128$-bit product. Consider a level with output range $[0\..m)$ and
$B$ buckets, whose slices have length $L=2^\ell$ (slice lengths are powers of
two; we discuss their choice below). Let $h$ be the hash of a key, and let
$\alpha=h/2^{64}\in[0,1)$. The bucket of the key is $\lfloor\alpha
B\rfloor$, which in fixed-point arithmetic is just $\hi(hB)$, and the start of
its slice is $\sigma(h)=\lfloor\alpha(m-L+1)\rfloor$, which is
$\hi(h(m-L+1))$, so that slices lie within the output range
(Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}). The assignment of buckets by a fixed-point
multiplication, which makes each bucket an interval of hashes, was introduced
by Genuzio, Ottaviano and Vigna for the offline bucketing of
GOV~\cite{GOVFSCCSMPHF}. Moreover, there
are $R$ \emph{layouts}, where $R=2^\rho$ is a power of two, each assigning
an in-slice offset to every key:
letting $o=\operatorname{lo}(hB)$, we divide $o$ into $R$ fields of
$f=64/R$ bits, and layout $r\in[0\..R)$ assigns to the key the offset
\[
\omega_r(o)=(o\gg rf) \bmod L,
\]
that is, the $\ell$ lowest bits of field $r$. We require $\ell\le f$, so
that the offsets of different layouts use disjoint bits of $o$.

The seed $s\in[1\..2^S)$ of a bucket has two parts: its lowest $\rho$ bits,
$r=s\bmod R$, choose a layout, and its remaining bits,
$j=s\gg\rho\in[0\..2^{S-\rho})$, a \emph{rotation}. Assuming for the
time being that $\ell\geq S$, let $u=2^{\ell-S}$: the seed maps each key of
the bucket to the slot
\[
\sigma(h)+\bigl(\omega_r(o)+su\bigr)\bmod L
=\sigma(h)+\bigl(\omega_r(o)+ru+jT\bigr)\bmod L,
\]
where $T=Ru$ is the \emph{stride}; the two forms are equal because $s=jR+r$,
and queries compute the first one. In words: once the layout is chosen, each
key of the bucket has a \emph{base slot}, the slot for rotation $0$ (as
offsets are uniform, the constant $ru$ is immaterial), and rotation~$j$ moves
all the keys of the bucket by $jT$ slots, wrapping around the end of their
slices. As the rotation varies, a key goes
exactly once through the $2^{S-\rho}=L/T$ slots of its slice whose distance
from its base slot is a multiple of $T$: we call these slots its \emph{ring}
in layout~$r$ (Figure~\ref{fig:placement}). Seed $0$, that is, layout $0$
with rotation $0$, marks bumped buckets, so layout $0$ has one rotation less.

Two keys of a bucket with the same base slot in a layout move together,
so they collide for all rotations but the few for which exactly one of them
wraps around (the slices of the keys of a bucket can start a few slots
apart): this is a \emph{self-collision}. Since the offsets of different
layouts use disjoint bits of $o$, self-collisions happen
independently in each layout: a bucket of size $k$ has a self-collision in
every layout with probability about $(k(k-1)/(2L))^R$, which is negligible
already for $R=2$. Moreover, the seeds of a layout move all the keys of the
bucket by the same amount, so they are strongly correlated, whereas seeds of
different layouts are (almost) independent.

The value $o$ costs nothing, as it is the other half of the product that
yields the bucket, and it is a good source of offsets: for the keys of bucket
$b$ we have $hB=b\cdot2^{64}+o$, so $o/2^{64}=\alpha B-b$ is the fractional
part of $\alpha B$, which is uniformly distributed if $h$ is. Moreover, the
offsets of the keys of a bucket are independent of each other for every
layout, which would not be true of offsets taken from the upper bits of $h$,
as the keys of a bucket share the upper ${\approx}\lg B$ bits of
$h$. We require the number $B$ of buckets to be odd: if $B$ is even, the
lowest bit of $o$, and thus of the offset of layout~$0$, is always zero,
whereas if $B$ is odd the lowest bits of $o$ are a bijective function of the
lowest bits of $h$.

\paragraph{Queries.}
A query computes $hB$, reads the seed $s$ of bucket $\hi(hB)$, and, unless
$s=0$, computes the first form of the formula above. There is no need to
extract $r$: since $sf=(jR+r)f=64j+rf$, we have $sf\bmod 64=rf$, and the
query computes
\[
\sigma(h)+\bigl((o\gg(sf\bmod 64))+su\bigr)\bmod L,
\]
where a single reduction modulo $L$ both extracts the offset $\omega_r(o)$
from the shifted $o$ and wraps the sum around the end of the slice.

\paragraph{Search.}
In a bit vector of the used slots, the bits of the slots of a ring would be
$T$ bits apart. We make them adjacent by storing the set of used slots by
residue classes modulo $T$, as suggested by Beling and Sanders~\cite{BeSPHast}
for \PHastP with wrapping, that is, as $T$ \emph{rows} in which bit $q$ of
row $\tau$ records whether slot $qT+\tau$ is used. All the slots of a
ring have the same residue modulo $T$ (wrapping around the end of a slice
does not change it, as $T$ divides $L$), so the \emph{bits of the ring} of a
key, which record whether the slots of its ring are used, are $L/T$
consecutive bits of a row. We number the slots of a ring from $0$ in order
of position in the slice, and we call \emph{ring position} of a key the
number $y$ of its base slot: rotation $j$ maps the key to slot
$(y+j)\bmod(L/T)$ of its ring. Thus, cyclically shifting the bits of the
ring so that bit $y$ becomes bit $0$
we obtain $L/T$ bits whose bit $j$ tells whether rotation $j$ maps the key to
a used slot, and combining them for all the keys of the bucket by a bitwise
\textsc{or} we obtain $L/T$ bits whose zeros are the \emph{free} rotations,
which map every key to a free slot (Figure~\ref{fig:search}). A free rotation
$j$ yields the feasible seed $jR+r$ unless two keys of the bucket are mapped
to the same slot, which is rare. For $S=8$ and $R=4$ a ring has
$2^{S-\rho}=64$ slots whatever the slice length, so its bits fill a word:
each layout requires just reading and cyclically shifting a word for each
key. Note that rotation $0$ of layout $0$ (seed $0$) is not available.

Among the free rotations of all layouts we choose, as the current version
of the reference implementation of \PHast~\cite{BelBRLPFSDS}, the one
minimizing the product of the distances of the slots of the keys from the
\emph{origin} of the bucket, which lies $95$ slots before the start of the
leftmost slice that a key of the bucket can have
(Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}). With respect to the sum of
the slots, which \PHast used originally~\cite{BeSPHast}, the product saves
about $0.02$ bits per key with 8-bit seeds and $0.03$ with 10-bit seeds. We
compute the logarithm of the product, that is, the sum of the base-2
logarithms of the distances, which we read, in fixed point with $24$
fractional bits, from a table: since it is a sum of integers, the cost of a
rotation does not depend on the order of the keys. Evaluating a free rotation
thus requires a table lookup for each key of the bucket. When a bucket has at
most $32$ free rotations, which is the common case, we evaluate all of them,
with a single loop over the free rotations of all layouts that contains no
other branch. When there are more free rotations (e.g., at the beginning of a
sweep) we use instead the fact that in a layout the cost increases with the
rotation, except at the rotations at which some key wraps around: thus, for a
bucket of size $k$ we need to consider only the first free rotation and, for
each key, the first free rotation not smaller than the one at which the key
wraps around, that is, at most $k+1$ free rotations per layout. Finally, when
marking the slots of the best free rotation as used we check that no two keys
of the bucket are mapped to the same slot, and otherwise we move to the next
best one.

\paragraph{Grouping and scheduling.}
The sweep needs the hashes of the keys of each bucket, but in no particular
order, and we do not want to keep more than one copy of them. Thus, we group
the buckets into at most $256$ \emph{parts}, each made of a power-of-two
number of consecutive buckets, and the keys into \emph{batches} of
consecutive keys. A thread computes the hashes of the keys of a batch, and
appends each hash to the \emph{region} associated with the batch and with the
part containing the bucket of the hash, recording the part in a byte: the
hashes in a region are thus in the order of the keys. Regions are sized
before computing the hashes, assuming that hashes are uniform, with a slack of
six standard deviations: the few hashes that do not fit their region, if any,
are kept separately.

A part is distributed into buckets only when a sweep needs it, into a buffer
of the sweep (the part is distributed first into smaller parts that fit the
cache, and then each smaller part into its buckets), so regions are never
modified. After the sweep, the keys of bumped buckets, which must be passed to
the next level, are found by walking again the keys of each batch in order:
the byte of a key gives its part, its hash is the next unread hash of the
region of the batch for that part, and a bit vector with a bit per bucket
tells whether the bucket of the hash has been bumped. Writing to at most $256$
regions at a time and working on smaller parts of about $150$\,KB is
efficient on any current architecture, and the construction needs, besides
the hashes, little more than a byte per key: in our experiments, about $11$
bytes per key overall (Section~\ref{sec:large}).

The priority of a bucket has the same form as in \PHast: a weight depending
on the size of the bucket, minus $1024$ times the index of the bucket. Since buckets enter the window in
order of index, the buckets of each size in it are in order of index, and the
bucket with the highest priority is the first bucket of some size. Thus,
instead of a priority queue we keep a first-in first-out queue for each size
(buckets with $64$ keys or more, which are extremely rare, are kept in a
heap): extracting the next bucket
requires scanning the first elements of a few queues, and adding a bucket
takes constant time. Finally, the set of used slots of the level, which is
needed to build the remapping, is a byproduct of the sweep: the sweep keeps in a cyclic
buffer only the bits of the slots that the buckets still to be placed can
reach, and copies the bits of the slots that are no longer reachable to the
bit vector of the level when their space in the buffer is recycled.

\paragraph{Slice length.}
The slice length of a level with output range $[0\..m)$ is the smallest power
of two larger than $m/2$, up to a maximum, which is a parameter: we use
$L=1024$ for $S=8$ and $L=2048$ for $S=10$. The
slices of small levels can thus have fewer slots than there are seeds, that
is, $\ell<S$: in this case we let $u=1$, so the stride is $T=R$, rings have
$L/R$ slots (a single slot if $L<R$), and rotations $j$ and $j'$ map the
keys of a bucket to the same slots whenever $L$ divides $(j-j')R$.

\paragraph{Levels.}
The following levels use the same placement scheme with output range equal
to their number of keys, and the remapping maps their slots to the holes of
the first level. A bumped key is hashed again just once, with a different
hash seed: if $h'$ is this \emph{second hash}, the hash of the key at level
$i>1$, which plays the role of $h$ at that level, is
\[
h_i=\hi(Y)\oplus\operatorname{lo}(Y),\quad\text{where $Y=(h\oplus z_i)(h'\oplus c)$},
\]
$z_i$ is a \emph{salt} associated with the level, and $c$ is a constant (we
let $h_1=h$). Thus, the following levels depend only on $h$ and $h'$: once $h'$ has
been computed the key is no longer needed, and the code accessing the
following levels, which takes just two integers, can be kept separate from
that accessing the first level.

\paragraph{Colliding hashes.}
Keys with the same $64$-bit hash at the first level have the same slice and
the same offsets in all layouts, so they collide for every seed and their
bucket is bumped; at the following levels their hashes depend also on the
second hash, and thus they are separated. Since about $n^2/2^{65}$ pairs of
keys have the same hash (about three for $n=10^{10}$), the keys bumped in this
way are negligible with respect to the $\beta n$ keys that are bumped anyway.
Hence, $64$-bit hashes are sufficient for any number of keys. Construction would fail only if two keys had the same pair of
hashes, which happens in practice only for duplicate keys. Since duplicate
keys are always bumped from the first level, to report them we examine the
buckets of the following levels: keys of a bucket with the same hash are
hashed again with a third hash seed, and keys whose hashes coincide again are considered
duplicates.

\paragraph{Last level.}
When at most $4096$ keys are left, a last level with $n'$ keys uses the same
placement scheme with an output range of $\lfloor 5n'/4\rfloor+16$ slots, expected
bucket size $\min(\lambda,3)$, and
no bumping, changing the salt (and, every eight
attempts, enlarging the output range) in the unlikely case of failure.

\paragraph{Parallel construction.}
As in \PHast, buckets are split into chunks (one for each thread, provided
that each chunk has at least $\max(4096,64g)$ buckets, where $g$ is the
length of a gap) separated by gaps of
\[
\left\lfloor \frac{LB}{m-L+1}\right\rfloor+1
\]
buckets: the slices of the keys of bucket $b$ start at about $b(m-L+1)/B$, and the slot
of a key lies at most $L-1$ slots after the start of its slice, so
(considering the rounding of slice starts) the keys of different chunks
cannot reach the same slots (in Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}, the brace spans
about as many buckets as a gap). Chunks are swept in parallel, and then all gaps
are swept in parallel, each with a new sweep in which the slots used by the
keys of the neighboring chunks (on both sides) are marked as used.

\section{Differences from \PHast and \PHastP}
\label{sec:differences}

In \PHast~\cite{BeSPHast}, in its variant \PHastP, and in \PHastR the bucket
of a key with hash $h$ is $\hi(hB)$, and the seed $s$ of the bucket maps the
key to slot $\sigma(h)+p(s,h)$, where $\sigma(h)$ is the start of its slice:
the three structures differ mainly in their placement scheme, that is, in the
function $p$. In \PHast, $p$ is a pseudorandom function, so finding the best
seed requires trying all seeds. \PHastP uses instead the \emph{additive
scheme} $p(s,h)=(h\bmod L)+s-1$, so that feasible seeds can be found by
combining, for each key, a word of the bit vector of used slots, but it needs
more space than \PHast; \PHastP
\emph{with wrapping} uses $p(s,h)=(h+\delta s)\bmod L$ for a small multiplier
$\delta$, so that keys do not leave their slice, and needs less space than
\PHastP without wrapping, but it is slower to build. In \PHastR,
$p(s,h)=\bigl(\omega_r(o)+su\bigr)\bmod L$, where $r=s\bmod R$ and
$o=\operatorname{lo}(hB)$.

\paragraph{The additive scheme is rigid.}
As in \PHastR, the first level of \PHastP has output range $m=n$, so its holes
are exactly its bumped keys, and by~(\ref{eq:space}) its excess space comes
from bumping. The additive scheme, however, moves all the keys of a bucket by
the same amount: two keys of a bucket that collide for one seed, which
happens with probability about $k(k-1)/(2L)$ for a bucket of size $k$,
collide for every seed (Beling and Sanders observe that in \PHastP the test for
self-collisions can thus be performed just once~\cite{BeSPHast}). Wrapping
does not change the picture, as two such keys move
together around their slices, and collide for all seeds but the few for
which exactly one of them has wrapped. Inspecting the first level of the
reference implementation, we found that these self-collisions cause a large
part of the bumping: with 8-bit seeds, wrapping and $\delta=3$, $35\%$ of the
bumped buckets contain one. Moreover, since each seed
moves all the keys of a bucket by the same amount, the seeds of a bucket are
strongly correlated; and the excess space cannot be recovered by compressing
the seeds, which are almost incompressible (the empirical entropy of the
8-bit seeds of the first level is $7.9$ bits).

\paragraph{Layouts.}
In \PHastR the seed first chooses among $R$ layouts, and then a rotation:
as self-collisions happen independently in each layout
(Section~\ref{sec:phastr}), buckets with a self-collision in every layout
are negligible (in Figure~\ref{fig:placement}, the keys $x_1$ and $x_2$
would collide for all seeds in \PHastP, but in \PHastR they collide only for
the seeds choosing layout~$0$). Queries need just two shifts
more than in \PHastP with wrapping, and wrapping does not complicate the
search: in \PHastP with wrapping the seeds must be split into intervals at
the points at which some key wraps, and with $\delta>1$ only one bit out of
$\delta$ of each word read from the bit vector is meaningful (Beling and
Sanders~\cite{BeSPHast} suggest that storing separately the slots with
different residues modulo $\delta$ could recover the lost parallelism),
whereas in
\PHastR the bits of the ring of a key are adjacent bits of a row, and
wrapping is taken care of by the cyclic shift.

\paragraph{Construction.}
The construction of \PHastR differs from that of the reference implementation
also in several engineering aspects (Section~\ref{sec:phastr}): hashes are
distributed into parts rather than sorted, the priority queue is replaced by
a queue for each bucket size, bumped keys are hashed again just once rather
than at each level, the last level uses the same scheme as the other levels
rather than the pseudorandom scheme of \PHast, and the gaps between the
chunks of a parallel construction are swept in parallel, as suggested by
Beling and Sanders~\cite{BeSPHast}, rather than serially. Slice lengths are those of \PHastP
with wrapping, but the expected bucket size is smaller, and the weights of
the bucket priorities are tuned for \PHastR.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We ran all experiments on an Amazon EC2 \texttt{c7i.metal-24xl} instance
(one Intel Xeon Platinum 8488C with 48 cores, 105\,MB of L3 cache and
192\,GiB of RAM), compiling with Rust 1.99 at optimization level 3, with thin
link-time optimization, a single code-generation unit, and code generation
for the native processor (\texttt{-C target-cpu=native}). Keys are distinct
64-bit integers, obtained by multiplying their index by an odd constant,
except at the end of this section, where they are strings; to make query times comparable, all
structures use the same hash function, GxHash with a hash seed. \PHastR uses
$R=4$ layouts, with byte seeds for $S=8$ and bit-packed seeds read without
alignment for $S=10$. Construction times include hashing; they are
single-threaded unless otherwise noted, and they are medians of three
constructions up to $10^8$ keys and of a single one on $10^9$ keys. Query
times are medians over nine rounds of $2\cdot10^6$ queries of consecutive
keys of the set, and the rounds of the different structures are interleaved.
Since all structures hash the key first, their memory accesses are random
anyway: reading the keys in order avoids adding to every query the same cache
miss on the array of keys.

The reference implementation of \PHast and \PHastP is the \texttt{ph} crate
by Beling~\cite{BelBRLPFSDS}, in the version of October 8, 2026 (commit
\texttt{aa497a8}): we use
\texttt{Function2} with the seed choosers \texttt{ShiftOnly} for \PHastP,
\texttt{ShiftOnlyProdWrapped<$\delta$>} for \PHastP with wrapping, and
\texttt{SeedOnly(ProdOfValues)} for \PHast. The last two choose among the
feasible seeds by the product of the distances of the slots, as \PHastR does,
whereas \texttt{ShiftOnly} takes the first feasible seed. We applied a fix
to the parallel construction of \texttt{ph} (commit \texttt{d0954d2} of the
fork used for our experiments): without it, all threads but the first choose
seeds using wrong bucket indices, and space grows with the number of
threads; single-threaded constructions are not affected.

We also compare with PtrHash~\cite{RagPTR} (version 2.1.2), in its two
minimal configurations: the \emph{default} one, with expected bucket size
$\lambda=3$, a linear assignment of keys to buckets, and a plain array for
the remapping, and the \emph{compact} one, with $\lambda=3.9$, a skewed
assignment of keys to buckets, and an Elias--Fano remapping. Both store
pilots in bytes and use about $1.01n$ slots, so a small fraction of the keys
must be remapped; the default configuration is built as a single part, and
its construction is thus almost sequential, whereas the compact one is built
in parallel parts. We measure the space of PtrHash as the space of its
pilots and of its remapping.

\paragraph{Trade-offs.}
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows what happens when the expected bucket size
varies around the configurations of the tables. Space has a minimum, query
time grows with $\lambda$, as more keys are bumped, and construction time
varies by at most $15\%$, except for \PHastR, whose construction becomes
slower as $\lambda$ decreases (by up to $34\%$). The curves of \PHastR are
slightly to the right of those of \PHast (their minima differ by about
$0.01$ bits per key), but their construction times differ by more than a
factor of ten, and the curves of \PHastR are below those of \PHast: at about
the same space, \PHastR has faster queries. The curves of \PHastP with
wrapping are to the right of both, even with $\delta=7$. Considering the
three dimensions at once, no configuration of \PHastP without wrapping, or
with wrapping and $\delta\geq2$, is Pareto-optimal: each is dominated by a
configuration of \PHastR with less space, faster queries and a faster
construction. On $10^7$ and $10^8$ keys a 10-bit configuration of \PHastP
with wrapping and $\delta=1$ (not plotted) is not dominated: it is built
faster (by less than $1\%$) than every 10-bit configuration of \PHastR, but
it uses more than $0.04$ bits per key more; on $10^9$ keys it is dominated
too. The only other configurations that are not dominated are some of those
of \PHast, which win by about $0.01$ bits per key in space or by less than
$1$\,ns in query time, at more than ten times the construction time, and, on
$10^9$ keys, the default configuration of PtrHash, whose queries are
$0.1$\,ns faster than those of the fastest configuration of \PHastR, at the
price of almost one bit per key more and five times the construction time.

\paragraph{Bumped keys.}
Table~\ref{tab:split} shows the time of queries for the keys of the first
level only, for four of the configurations of the previous tables. It is
essentially the same for \PHastP with wrapping and for \PHastR, as it is
dominated by the access to the seed, and it grows with the size of the array
of seeds; it is slightly larger for the reference implementation of \PHast,
which explains part of its gap. Besides that, the differences between the
structures are due to bumped keys. In a workload in which all keys are queried, a query
for a bumped key takes $35$--$50$\,ns more than a query for a key of the
first level on $10^7$ keys, $130$--$410$\,ns more on $10^8$ keys, and
$240$--$650$\,ns more on $10^9$ keys: besides accessing another level and
the remapping, a bumped key causes the misprediction of the test
on the seed, which interrupts the overlapped execution of the following
queries. Thus, each percent of bumped keys costs on average up to half a
nanosecond per query on
$10^7$ keys, more than $1$\,ns on $10^8$ keys, and more than $2$\,ns on
$10^9$ keys: the larger the key set, the more a small expected bucket size
is convenient. This is the reason why we suggest $\lambda=4.25$: with
$\lambda=4.5$ \PHastR uses $0.04$ bits per key less, and it is built faster,
but on $10^9$ keys its queries are $8\%$ slower.

\paragraph{Number of layouts.}
Table~\ref{tab:layouts} isolates the effect of layouts, comparing
structures that differ only in their number. With 8-bit seeds and the
default expected bucket size, going from one to four layouts reduces the
bumped keys from $1.8\%$ to $0.5\%$ and saves $0.12$ bits per key; queries
become $13\%$ faster with the same code, and another $16\%$ faster with the
code that \PHastR uses with four layouts, in which the shifts are constants,
so that the compiler can fuse the scaling of the seed with the addition.
The advantage does not depend on the choice of the expected bucket size:
when each number of layouts uses the expected bucket size that minimizes its
space, four layouts still save $0.055$ bits per key with respect to one,
and they are faster both to build and to query. Four layouts are also the
fastest to build, as only with four layouts the bits of the ring of a key
fill a single word (Section~\ref{sec:phastr}). With 10-bit seeds the
reduction is smaller (from $1.4\%$ to $0.7\%$ of bumped keys, saving $0.06$
bits per key, and $0.03$ when each number of layouts uses its best expected
bucket size), and the construction with four layouts is $6$--$7\%$ slower
than with two.
Self-collisions explain most of the reduction: with $S=8$ and a single
layout, the buckets containing a self-collision hold $1.28\%$ of the keys, in
agreement with the first-order estimate $\lambda(\lambda+2)/(2L)\approx
1.30\%$, and they contain $71\%$ of the bumped keys; with two layouts, the
buckets with a self-collision in both layouts hold just $0.03\%$ of the keys,
and with four layouts there are none, so the further reduction from two to
four layouts is due to the larger number of independent placements.

\paragraph{Construction.}
The construction time per key of \PHastR grows slowly with the number of
keys ($54$, $60$ and $63$\,ns with one thread on $10^7$, $10^8$ and $10^9$
keys, respectively), as parts are distributed into smaller parts that fit
the cache whatever the number of keys (Section~\ref{sec:phastr}); the growth
is due to the walk finding the keys of bumped buckets, which accesses
randomly the bit vector of bumped buckets. Table~\ref{tab:threads} shows how
construction scales with the number of threads: with $32$ threads \PHastR is
built $20$ times faster than with one, whereas \PHastP is built $9$ times
faster, and \PHastP with wrapping $13$ times faster; the construction of
\PHastP stops getting faster at about $7$\,ns per key. With all the hardware
threads of the machine, the structure on $10^9$ keys is built in $2.6$\,ns
per key, $2.7$ times faster than \PHastP and $2.8$ times faster than \PHastP
with wrapping; \PHast, whose construction time is dominated by the search of
seeds, has the largest speedup, but it is still more than six times slower.
The compact configuration of PtrHash scales well, too ($22$ times faster
with $32$ threads), but with all threads it is still $2.4$ times slower than
\PHastR; the default one, which is built as a single part, does not scale.
The speedup of \PHastR is limited by the phases that are bound by memory
bandwidth and latency, that is, hashing, the distribution into parts, and
the walk. The peak memory usage is close to that of
the reference implementation of \PHastP, which sorts hashes in place and
hashes all keys again to find those of bumped buckets.

\paragraph{String keys.}
On random strings of $10$ to $50$ bytes, hashed with GxHash by both
implementations, hashing takes a larger part of the time, and since the
reference implementation hashes all keys again to find those of bumped
buckets, \PHastR is built faster than \PHastP also with one thread; otherwise,
the relative performance of the structures does not change, and neither does
space: on $10^8$ keys, with 8-bit seeds and $\lambda=4.25$, \PHastR is built
in $72$\,ns per key, against the $86$, $119$ and $705$\,ns of \PHastP,
\PHastP with wrapping and $\delta=3$, and \PHast, and its queries take
$25.5$\,ns, against $34.1$, $29.3$ and $26.9$\,ns.

\section{Offline Construction}
\label{sec:offline}

The construction of Section~\ref{sec:phastr} keeps in memory the hashes of all
keys and, as bumped keys are hashed again, the keys themselves: about $11$
bytes per key besides the keys. To build structures on hundreds of billions
of keys, \PHastR has also an offline construction, which reads the keys just
once, keeps their hashes on disk, needs in memory, besides $256$\,MiB of
buffers, less than half a byte per key, and builds exactly the same
structure. The external-memory construction of
PTHash~\cite{PTPEMCMPHFP} also builds the same structure as its construction
in memory, but since PTHash processes buckets in order of decreasing size, it
must sort the hashes by bucket on disk and group the buckets by size. Beling
and Sanders~\cite{BeSPHast} outline, but do not implement, an external-memory
construction of \PHast, which also sorts the hashes by bucket on disk, and
reads the keys twice, the second time to find the keys of bumped buckets. In
\PHastR, instead, buckets enter the sweep in order of index, so it is
sufficient to distribute the hashes into partitions. Distributing the hashes
on disk by their highest bits has been a standard technique of scalable
constructions of static functions and MPHFs since~\cite{BPZPPHNOS}, but
these constructions build a separate structure for each partition, whereas
in \PHastR the linear map from hashes to buckets and the sweep in order of
index make it possible to build in this way a single structure, identical to
the one built in memory.

\paragraph{Records.}
The hash $h_i$ of a key at every level depends only on its hash~$h$ and on
its second hash~$h'$ (Section~\ref{sec:phastr}).
Thus, while reading the keys we compute both hashes of every key, and write a
$16$-byte \emph{record} $(h,h')$ to a temporary file: the keys are no longer
necessary, and they can be streamed, for example, from a file. The records of
a level are distributed into $P$ \emph{partitions} by the upper $\lg P$ bits
of the hash of the key at the level. Each partition is a sequence of
blocks: the blocks of all partitions are appended to the same file as their
buffers fill, and a table locates the blocks of each partition. A writer
starts with blocks of $1024$ records, and doubles their length (up to
$65\,536$ records, that is, $1$\,MiB) whenever it has written on average
$512$ blocks for each partition: on huge key sets a sweep reads a partition
with few seeks, and the buffers, a block for each partition, grow slowly
with the number of keys. For the first level, whose number of keys is not
known in advance, $P=2^{14}$: the buffers take $256$\,MiB up to about
$8.6\cdot10^9$ keys, and at most $16$\,GiB, and partitions contain just a
few million keys even on $10^{11}$ keys. At the following levels, whose number~$n'$ of keys is known, $P$ is the
smallest power of two not smaller than $\sqrt{2n'/1024}$: this choice balances
the memory of a thread writing the file, which buffers a block for each
partition, and that of a thread sweeping the level, which buffers a few
partitions.

\paragraph{Parts.}
Since $\hi(hB)$ is a nondecreasing function of $h$, the keys of a partition
belong to consecutive buckets, and only the first and the last of these
buckets can contain also keys of other partitions. Offline, the parts
(Section~\ref{sec:phastr}), which sweeps load in increasing order, are made
of consecutive partitions containing together at least $512$ buckets, and a
bucket whose keys lie in two parts belongs to the second one: when a sweep
loads a part, it sets aside the keys found in the part that belong to the
first bucket of the following part, and adds them to that part when it loads
it. Thus, each partition is read once by each sweep, except at the beginning
of a sweep, where the previous part might need to be read too.

\paragraph{Same structure.}
Given the slots already used, the seed chosen for a bucket depends only on
the set of the hashes of its keys, and not on their order: free rotations are
computed by a bitwise \textsc{or}, the cost of a rotation is a sum of
integers read from a table (Section~\ref{sec:phastr}), and ties are broken in
favor of the first layout and, within a layout, of the first rotation. Since the sweeps see the same
buckets in the same order, the structure built offline is identical to that
built in memory, provided that the number of threads is the same (it
determines the chunks of a parallel construction). The only difference is in
the detection of duplicate keys (Section~\ref{sec:phastr}): as the keys are no
longer available for a third hash, offline we consider duplicates two keys
with the same record. The two criteria differ only in the presence of two
distinct keys with the same $128$ bits of hashes, in which case both
constructions fail.

\paragraph{Following levels and memory.}
After the sweep of a level, its file is read again, and the records of the
keys of bumped buckets are written to the file of the following
level, or kept in memory if they are at most $4096$: the file of the first
level is written once and read twice, and the following files are much
smaller. In memory, besides buffers whose size grows slowly with the number
of keys, the sweep of the first level needs its seeds, as bytes when they
have at most eight bits, and its set of used slots, which the sweeps of a
parallel construction update directly, as each sets only the bits of its
slots: with $S=8$ and $\lambda=4.25$, about $1/4.25+1/8\approx0.36$ bytes
per key. Then, the seeds of the first level become those of the structure,
its free slots are kept in an Elias--Fano sequence, and the remapping is
built at the end from the sets of used slots of the following levels, which
are small: from this point on, memory is essentially that of the structure
being built.

\paragraph{Experiments.}
On $10^9$ keys the offline construction allocates at most $0.38$ bytes per
key with one thread and $0.41$ with eight, against $9.9$ and $11.4$ bytes
per key, excluding the keys, of the construction in memory (as measured by a
counting allocator, which counts also memory that is allocated but never
used: the resident set size of the construction in memory with eight
threads is $10.6$ bytes per key, Table~\ref{tab:threads}). It takes $64.6$\,ns per key with one thread and $18.8$
with eight, against $62.6$ and $9.5$: with one thread the difference is
small, but with eight the time is dominated by the first phase, in which the
keys are read, and the records are written, by a single thread. On
$10^{11}$ keys, the offline construction would need about $40$\,GB of memory
and $1.6$\,TB of disk,
whereas the construction in memory would need more than $1$\,TB of memory
besides the keys.

\section{The Limits of the Approach}
\label{sec:limits}

The first level of \PHastR still bumps between $0.5\%$ and $1.2\%$ of the
keys with the suggested expected bucket sizes, and bumping is where the
excess space goes: it is natural to ask how much a more flexible placement
scheme, or a more powerful search, such as the backtracking and the local
search suggested by Beling and Sanders~\cite{BeSPHast}, could reduce it. In
this section we show that bumping cannot be avoided, for a structural reason
that applies to every placement scheme of the \PHast family, and we compute
how many keys must be bumped: on large key sets, about $n/(2W)$, that is,
a fraction $1/(2W)$ of the keys, where $W$ is the number of slots in which a
key can be placed. For \PHastR this is about one tenth of the keys that it bumps.

In all structures of the \PHast family a key whose slice starts at $\sigma$
can be placed only in its \emph{reach} $[\sigma\..\sigma+W)$, where $W=L$ for
\PHast, for \PHastP with wrapping, and for \PHastR, and $W=L+2^S-2$ for
\PHastP without wrapping. The seed of a bucket chooses the slots of its keys
within their reaches, but the reaches themselves depend only on the hashes of
the keys. This simple constraint is sufficient to force bumping.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:bump}
Consider $n$ keys and $n$ slots, and assume that, for some $W\geq 1$, each key
$x$ can be placed only in the slots $[\sigma_x\..\sigma_x+W)$. Let
\[
X(t) = t - \bigl|\{\,x \mid \sigma_x < t\,\}\bigr|\qquad (0\leq t\leq n),
\]
and let $D=\max_t X(t)-\min_t X(t)$ be the range of $X$. Then, every injective
placement of a subset of the keys bumps (i.e., leaves out) at least $D-W+1$
keys.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $0\leq t\leq t'\leq n$ the number of keys $x$ with $t\leq\sigma_x<t'$ is
$t'-t-\bigl(X(t')-X(t)\bigr)$. Only these keys can be placed in the $t'-t-W+1$
slots in $[t+W-1\..t')$, so at least $X(t')-X(t)-W+1$ of these slots are free.
Symmetrically, these keys can be placed only in the at most $t'-t+W-1$ slots
in $[t\..t'+W-1)$, so at least $X(t)-X(t')-W+1$ of them are bumped. Since there
are as many keys as slots, the number of bumped keys is equal to the number of
free slots, and it is thus at least $\bigl|X(t')-X(t)\bigr|-W+1$ for all
$t\leq t'$. Choosing as $t$ and $t'$ the points at which $X$ attains its
minimum and its maximum, in the appropriate order, completes the proof.
\end{proof}

The proposition is a quantitative version of the (easy) necessity of Hall's
condition~\cite{HalORS} for the bipartite graph connecting each key to the
slots of its reach. In the \PHast family slice starts are computed from a
hash by a multiplication in fixed-point arithmetic, so we can model them as
independent and uniformly distributed. If the $\sigma_x$ are independent and
uniform in $[0\..n)$, then $D=nV_n-1$, where $V_n$ is Kuiper's
statistic~\cite{KuiTCRPC} of the values $\sigma_x/n$, that is, the sum of the
maximum positive and negative deviations of their empirical distribution from
the uniform distribution (the compression of slice starts into
$[0\..n-W+1)$ changes $D$ by at most $W-1$, which does not affect the
asymptotics). By Donsker's theorem~\cite{BilCPM} and the continuity of the
range, $D/\sqrt n$ converges in distribution to the range of a standard
Brownian bridge (which, by Vervaat's transformation~\cite{VerRBBBE}, has the
same distribution as the maximum of a standard Brownian
excursion~\cite{KenDMBE}). By symmetry, the mean of the range is twice the
mean of the maximum of the bridge, which exceeds $x\geq0$ with probability
$e^{-2x^2}$~\cite{BilCPM}, so it is $2\int_0^\infty
e^{-2x^2}\,dx=\sqrt{\pi/2}$. Since $V_n$ is at most twice the maximum absolute
deviation of the empirical distribution from the uniform distribution, the
Dvoretzky--Kiefer--Wolfowitz inequality~\cite{MasTCDKWI} shows that the tails
of $D/\sqrt n$ are sub-Gaussian, uniformly in $n$; thus, the convergence
extends to expectations, and we conclude that
\[
\mathbf E[D]=\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}2}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).
\]

Proposition~\ref{prop:bump} uses a single interval of slots, the one between
the points at which $X$ attains its minimum and its maximum, and thus it
accounts only for the largest imbalance between slots and keys: it cannot
exceed the range of $X$, which grows only as $\sqrt n$, and it is a good
estimate only as long as $\sqrt{\pi n/2}$ is not much larger than $W$, that
is, up to a few million keys when $W=1024$. However, the argument of its
proof applies to every interval of slots $[t\..t')$, and to every placement:
if $X$ increases by $\Delta$ on $[t\..t')$, at least $\Delta-W+1$ slots in
$[t\..t')$ are free, and if $X$ decreases by $\Delta$, at least
$\Delta-W+1$ keys whose reach starts in $[t\..t')$ are bumped. When $X$
oscillates, several intervals have a large imbalance, and each forces its own
free slots or bumped keys (Figure~\ref{fig:intervals}). If the intervals of
slots $[t_1\..t'_1)$, $[t_2\..t'_2)$, $\ldots$ are pairwise disjoint, a free
slot lies in at most one of them, and the reach of a bumped key starts in at
most one of them, so the free slots and the bumped keys counted for
different intervals are distinct: the bumped keys are at least the sum of
the bounds given by the intervals on which $X$ decreases, and the free slots,
which are as many, at least the sum of the bounds given by the intervals on
which $X$ increases. Thus, at least
\[
\frac12\sum_i\Bigl(\bigl|X(t'_i)-X(t_i)\bigr|-W+1\Bigr)^+
\]
keys are bumped, where $x^+=\max(x,0)$. The increment of $X$ on an interval
of $\ell$ slots is asymptotically normal with mean zero and variance $\ell$,
so applying the bound to the $n/\ell$ consecutive intervals of $\ell$ slots
shows that, for large $W$, the expected number of bumped keys is at least
about $\bigl(n/(2\ell)\bigr)\mathbf E\bigl[(\sqrt\ell\,|Z|-W)^+\bigr]$, where $Z$
is a standard normal variable. Letting $\ell=W^2/a^2$, this is
$a\bigl(\varphi(a)-a(1-\Phi(a))\bigr)n/W$, where $\varphi$ and $\Phi$ are
the density and the distribution function of $Z$; its maximum, attained for
$a\approx0.61$, that is, for intervals of about $2.7W^2$ slots, is about
\[
0.1\,\frac nW,
\]
a constant fraction of the keys, however large the key set.

The minimum number of bumped keys can actually be computed exactly:

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:greedy}
Under the assumptions of Proposition~\ref{prop:bump}, the minimum number of
keys bumped by an injective placement is the number of keys bumped by the
following greedy placement: for each slot, in increasing order, place in the
slot the unplaced key with the smallest $\sigma_x$ among those whose
reach contains the slot, if any.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $G$ be the greedy placement and $P$ any placement. We show by induction
on $t$ that there is a placement $Q$ that places at least as many keys as $P$
and agrees with $G$ on the slots in $[0\..t)$, that is, places the same keys
in them. For $t=0$ we take $Q=P$; for $t=n$, $Q$ places the same keys as
$G$, which thus places at least as many keys as $P$.

Let $Q$ agree with $G$ on $[0\..t)$. If $G$ leaves slot $t$ free, every key
whose reach contains $t$ is placed by $G$, and thus by $Q$, before $t$, so
$Q$ leaves slot $t$ free, too. Otherwise, let $g$ be the key placed by $G$ in
slot $t$, which is not placed by $Q$ before $t$. If $Q$ places $g$ in slot
$t$, there is nothing to do. If $Q$ leaves slot $t$ free, we move $g$ to
slot $t$ (or add it, if $Q$ bumps it). Otherwise, $Q$ places in slot $t$ a
key $h\neq g$, which is not placed by $G$ before $t$, so $\sigma_g\leq\sigma_h$
by the greedy choice. If $Q$ bumps $g$, we replace $h$ with $g$; if $Q$
places $g$ in a slot $u>t$, we exchange $g$ and $h$, as $h$ can be placed
in slot $u$: $\sigma_h\leq t<u<\sigma_g+W\leq\sigma_h+W$. In all cases the
number of placed keys does not decrease, the slots in $[0\..t)$ are
unchanged, and the new placement agrees with $G$ on $[0\..t]$.
\end{proof}

The greedy placement is a special case of Glover's algorithm for maximum
matchings in convex bipartite graphs~\cite{GloMMCBG}, which assigns each
slot to the unassigned key whose interval ends first (as all reaches have
the same length, the reach that ends first is the one that starts first).
Concretely, it is a queue that at each slot receives the keys whose slice
starts there, places the oldest waiting key, and bumps the keys that have
waited $W$ slots. On average one key arrives at each slot, so the length
of the queue behaves like a random walk with no drift and unit variance per
step, reflected at zero and at $W$. In the stationary regime the variance
added by the arrivals must be balanced by the losses at $W$, each of which
reduces the second moment of the length by about $2W$: thus, keys are lost at
a rate of about $1/(2W)$ per slot, and on large key sets the minimum number
of bumped keys is about
\[
\frac n{2W},
\]
that is, $0.049\%$ of the keys when $W=1024$. Table~\ref{tab:floor} shows
that this estimate is accurate on the slice starts of our key sets: on
$10^9$ keys the minimum computed by the greedy placement is $0.050\%$ of the
keys, whereas Proposition~\ref{prop:bump} guarantees $0.003\%$.

In terms of space, this floor is negligible: by~(\ref{eq:space}), bumping a
fraction $\beta\approx1/(2W)$ of the keys costs about
$\beta(2+\lg(1/\beta))\approx0.006$ bits per key when $W=1024$. Its
importance lies in the fact that it applies to \emph{every} placement:
beyond a few million keys, no amount of search can make a first level with
$m=n$ free of bumping. Consistently, the variant of \PHast without bumping of
the reference implementation (\texttt{NBFunction}) can be built in practice
only with an output range larger than the number of keys, that is, when the
function is not minimal: with $(1+\varepsilon)n$ slots the queue above has a
negative drift, and its losses decrease exponentially with $\varepsilon W$.
On the other hand, \PHastR bumps about ten times as many keys as the reaches
force: its bumping is due to the constraints on the seeds, which offer at most
$2^S-1$ placements of the keys of a bucket, and not to the reaches. By~(\ref{eq:space}),
a search reaching the floor would reduce the space of \PHastR with $S=8$ and
$\lambda=4.25$ from $1.94$ to about $1.89$ bits per key: this is the room
that is left for better searches.

In particular, the floor rules out the possibility of obtaining a bump-free
version of \PHastR, whose space would be just $S/\lambda$ bits per key, by a
backtracking search in the style of CONSENSUS~\cite{LSWCSESAMPH}, which
combines the search and the encoding of seeds to get arbitrarily close to the
lower bound for MPHFs. For such a search to succeed, keys should be first
split into parts of exactly known size $C$, small enough for the reaches not
to force bumping, that is, with $\sqrt{\pi C/2}$ significantly smaller than
$W$, as happens (albeit for different reasons) in Bucketed
CONSENSUS-RecSplit~\cite{LSWCSESAMPH}, whose first step is a minimal
$k$-perfect hash function.

Even within such parts, however, a second obstacle appears. Whether a slot
is used depends on the buckets whose slices start at most $W$ slots before
it, that is, on about $W/\lambda$ consecutive buckets, and the slot becomes
unreachable only after all of them have been placed. Thus, a depth-first
search whose only failure test is that a free slot has become unreachable
must exhaust the alternatives of about $W/\lambda$ buckets before it can
change the choice that caused the failure (stronger propagation could detect
some failures earlier, but we did not try it). In a prototype using chained
seeds as in CONSENSUS (the offsets of a bucket depend on the seeds of the
previous eight buckets, so that backtracking rerandomizes the following
ones), a cyclic output range of $10^5$ slots to avoid boundary effects, and a
check that all unreachable slots are used, the search never got past the
first $160$ buckets, even with seeds more than two bits longer than the
information-theoretic minimum for a bucket ($S=8$, $L=512$, $R=4$, and
$\lambda=4$) and a budget of $2000$ seeds tried per bucket.

\section{Conclusions}

In \PHastR the lowest bits of a seed choose one of a few layouts, which are
independent of one another,
like the seeds of the pseudorandom scheme of \PHast, and whose offsets come
for free with the computation of the bucket; the remaining bits choose a
rotation, which moves all the keys of the bucket by the same amount, like
the seeds of the additive scheme of \PHastP. Rotations
keep the search bit-parallel, as the free rotations of a layout are found by
cyclic shifts of the bits of the rings of the keys, which are consecutive bits
once the set of used slots is stored by residue classes. Layouts, in turn,
remove the self-collisions of the additive scheme, which, even with wrapping,
cause a large part of the bumping of \PHastP; and
bumping is where both the excess space and the slow queries come from:
queries for the keys of the first level take the same time in \PHastR and in
\PHastP with wrapping, and the whole difference is due to bumped keys
(Table~\ref{tab:split}). As a result, with the same space as the best
variant of \PHastP with wrapping, \PHastR is built in $42\%$ of the time, and
its queries are $28\%$ faster on $10^9$ keys
(Tables~\ref{tab:1e7}--\ref{tab:1e9}); they are about as fast as those of
PtrHash, which uses more than one bit per key more; with a larger expected
bucket size,
it comes within $0.02$ bits per key of the space of \PHast, which takes more
than ten times as long to build, and still has faster queries; with many
threads it is built almost three times faster than \PHastP
(Table~\ref{tab:threads}); and every configuration of \PHastP in
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} is dominated by one of \PHastR (only an unplotted
configuration with $\delta=1$ and 10-bit seeds is not, on $10^7$ and $10^8$
keys). There are, however, intrinsic limits to how few keys can be
bumped: when each key can be placed only within a range of $W$ slots, every
placement of random keys must bump a fraction of about $1/(2W)$ of the keys
on large key sets, although this floor is about ten times lower than the
bumping of \PHastR. Finally, an offline
construction builds the same structure keeping the hashes of the keys on disk,
with less than half a byte per key of memory on large key sets.

There are several open problems and further research directions. On the
practical side, the geometry of small levels should be tuned for rings,
access to bit-packed seeds should be made faster, and the
last levels could be replaced by a more compact structure. More interestingly,
\PHastR bumps about ten times as many keys as the reaches force: whether a
search can approach this floor efficiently (which would save about $0.05$
bits per key), and whether bump-free placements, which would use just
$S/\lambda$ bits per key, can be found efficiently within parts of exactly
known size, in spite of the long-range constraints that defeat simple
backtracking, are open problems.
