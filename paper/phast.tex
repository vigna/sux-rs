\begin{abstract}
\PHast and its fast-to-build variant \PHastP are minimal perfect hash functions
with the fastest known queries: a key is mapped to a bucket by a linear
function of its hash, and a fixed-width per-bucket seed selects the positions
of the keys of the bucket inside small overlapping slices of the output range.
\PHastP uses additive placement, so that feasible seeds can be found with
bit-parallel operations, but it needs about $0.2$ more bits per key than
\PHast. We analyze where this space goes: with a minimal output range, every
bumped key leaves a hole that must be encoded, and the rigidity of additive
placement both forces more bumping and causes \emph{self-collisions} that no
seed can resolve. We then propose \PHastR, in which the seed selects one of $R$
independent offset patterns in addition to a shift, and in which construction
\emph{repairs} failing buckets by cuckoo-style eviction, detecting with a
bit-parallel half adder the shifts at which exactly one key is blocked.
Queries perform the same operations as in \PHastP, plus two shifts and a
multiplication that does not depend on the access to the seed. With 8-bit seeds, \PHastR matches the space
of \PHast (1.92 bits per key) while building $4.4$ times faster; with 10-bit
seeds it reaches 1.85 bits per key, the space of \PHast, $12$ times faster, and
improves on all \PHastP variants at the same construction time. Finally, we
study the limits of repair: we prove that when keys can move only within
windows of $W$ slots no placement of $n$ keys on $n$ slots, however found, can
avoid bumping about $\sqrt{\pi n/2}-W$ keys, and we show that most failures of
the sweep cannot be repaired at any depth, which explains why shallow repairs
are sufficient.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A \emph{minimal perfect hash function} (MPHF) maps a static set of $n$ keys
bijectively to $[0\..n)$; it requires at least $\lg e\approx 1.443$
bits per key (we use $\lg$ for the binary logarithm and $\ln$ for the natural
logarithm). Among the many practical constructions, \emph{bucket placement}
gives the fastest queries: keys are hashed to buckets, and a per-bucket seed
chooses positions for the keys of the bucket so that no two keys collide.

\PHast~\cite{BeSPHast} pushes this approach to its limit. A key with hash $c$
goes to bucket $\lfloor c B/2^{64}\rfloor$; the seed $s$ of the bucket, stored
in a plain array of $S$-bit values, places the key at
$\lfloor c(m-L+1)/2^{64}\rfloor + p(s,c)$, that is, inside a \emph{slice} of
length $L$ whose start grows linearly with $c$. Buckets for which no seed works
are \emph{bumped} (seed 0) and handled by further levels; an Elias--Fano
sequence~\cite{EliESRCASF,FanNBRISM} maps their outputs to the free slots of the
first level. A query thus reads a single seed for almost all keys. Seeds are
assigned by a sweep over the buckets that keeps a small window of buckets in a
priority queue, favoring large buckets, and chooses for each bucket the
feasible seed that minimizes the sum of the positions of its keys.

In \PHast, $p(s,c)$ is a pseudorandom function, so finding the best seed means
trying all $2^S-1$ of them; construction takes hundreds of nanoseconds per key.
\PHastP uses instead the additive placement $p(s,c)=(c \bmod L)+s-1$: the
feasibility of $64$ consecutive seeds of a bucket is the complement of the
bitwise \textsc{or} of $64$-bit windows of the occupancy bitmap, one per key,
and the best seed is the first feasible one. Construction becomes an order of
magnitude faster, but with 8-bit seeds space grows from 1.92 to 2.12 bits per
key; \emph{wrapping} variants partially close the gap at the cost of slower
construction.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item an analysis of the space of \PHastP showing that its excess comes from
bumping, and that a large part of it is caused by self-collisions inherent to
additive placement (Section~\ref{sec:anatomy});
\item \PHastR, a structure in which the seed chooses one of $R$ independent
offset patterns and a shift, and whose construction repairs failing buckets
by evicting the owner of the only blocking slot, detected by a bit-parallel
half adder (Section~\ref{sec:phastr});
\item an implementation in the Rust crate \texttt{sux} and an experimental
comparison with the reference implementation of \PHast and \PHastP
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments});
\item a lower bound on the number of bumped keys that holds for every placement
in which keys move within windows of fixed length, and an analysis of the
limits of repair, showing that most failures of the sweep cannot be repaired
at any depth, and that repair makes the sweep fail more often
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{itemize}

\section{The Space of \PHastP}
\label{sec:anatomy}

All measurements in this section refer to $10^7$ random keys, 8-bit seeds,
expected bucket size $\lambda=5.25$ and slice length $L=512$, the
space-optimal configuration of \PHastP. They have been obtained on the
reference implementation of \PHastP (the \texttt{ph} crate, using GxHash as
hash function), whose first level we inspect through read-only accessors.

\paragraph{Holes are bumped keys.}
The first level of \PHastP has output range $m=n$. Every key is either placed
in a distinct slot or bumped, so the number of free slots (\emph{holes}) is
exactly the number of bumped keys. Each hole costs about
$2+\lg(1/\beta)$ bits in the Elias--Fano remapping, where $\beta$ is the
fraction of bumped keys, and each bumped key must be placed again by the
following levels. With $S$-bit seeds the space is thus approximately
\[
\frac{S}{\lambda(1-\beta)} + \beta\Bigl(2+\lg\frac1\beta\Bigr),
\]
and since the entropy of a random set of $\beta n$ holes is already about
$\beta n(\lg(1/\beta)+\lg e)$, the encoding of holes cannot be
substantially improved: \emph{the only lever is $\beta$}. In our
configuration $\beta=7.6\%$, and bumping costs $0.59$ of the $2.12$ bits per
key ($0.47$ for Elias--Fano, $0.13$ for the further levels). We will show in Section~\ref{sec:limits} that on large key
sets no placement, however found, can avoid bumping altogether.

\paragraph{Additive placement is rigid.}
Additive placement translates a fixed pattern: two keys of a bucket whose base
positions coincide collide for every seed. For a bucket of size $k$ this
happens with probability about $k(k-1)/(2L)$, so a fraction
$\lambda^2/(2L)\approx 2.7\%$ of the buckets cannot be placed at all
(we measure $2.6\%$); they are $38.5\%$ of the bumped buckets and contain
about $(\lambda^2+2\lambda)/(2L)\approx 3.7\%$ of the keys (we measure
$3.6\%$). Moreover,
consecutive shifts are strongly correlated trials. Seeds, on the other hand,
are almost incompressible: their empirical entropy is $7.71$ bits, because
the shift mostly encodes how far the leftmost key of the bucket lies behind
the full part of its slice.

\paragraph{Placement.}
Each key has a 64-bit hash $h$, from which we derive a second 64-bit value $o$
(see below). Let $R=2^\rho$ be the number of
\emph{patterns}, $L=2^\ell$ the slice length with $\ell\le 64/R$, and
$D=2^S/R$ the number of shifts. The bucket of a key is
$\hi(hB)$ and the start of its slice is $\sigma(h)=\hi(h(m-L-D+2))$, where
$\hi(x)$ denotes the 64 most significant bits of a 128-bit product. The
$r$-th pattern assigns to the key the in-slice offset
\[
\omega_r(o)=\bigl\lfloor o/2^{64r/R}\bigr\rfloor \bmod L,
\]
that is, the $\ell$ bits of $o$ starting at bit $64r/R$: different patterns
are independent. A seed $s\in[1\..2^S)$ encodes the pair $r=s\bmod R$,
$d=\lfloor s/R\rfloor$ (the pair $r=d=0$ is not available), and the key is
mapped to
\[
\sigma(h)+\omega_r(o)+d.
\]
In other words, whereas in \PHastP all seeds translate the same rigid pattern
of base positions, in \PHastR the seed first chooses among $R$ independent
patterns, and then translates the chosen one (Figure~\ref{fig:placement}).
As in \PHast, seed $0$ means that the bucket is bumped. A query computes the
same quantities as in \PHastP, plus two shifts and the multiplication
deriving $o$ (see below), which is independent of the access to the seed. The encoding
makes it unnecessary to extract $r$: since $(s\cdot 64/R)\bmod 64=r\cdot 64/R$,
$\omega_r(o)$ is obtained by shifting $o$ right by $s\cdot 64/R$ modulo $64$
and masking, whereas $d$ is $s$ shifted right by $\rho$ bits. We set
$o=h\cdot c \bmod 2^{64}$ for an odd constant $c$. Keys of the same bucket share the
upper ${\approx}\lg B$ bits of $h$, and their slices start at about the same
position; but bit $j$ of $o$ depends on all bits $0$, \dots, $j$ of $h$, so
every offset depends on the lower bits of $h$, which vary independently among
the keys of a bucket. (Since $\omega_{R-1}$ uses bits of $o$ up to $63$, taking
$o=h$ would make the last pattern nearly constant within a bucket.) With $R=1$ and $o=h$, \PHastR is essentially \PHastP.

\paragraph{Search.}
For each pattern, the best shift is found as in \PHastP: the occupancy words
starting at the base positions $\sigma(h_i)+\omega_r(o_i)$ of the $k$ keys are
\textsc{or}ed, and the first zero bit is the smallest feasible shift
(Figure~\ref{fig:search}(a)); for
$S=8$ and $R=4$ we have $D=64$, so one word per key suffices. Among patterns, we keep the one minimizing
the sum of positions, skipping a pattern as soon as the sum of its bases
exceeds the best sum found. Since all $R$ patterns of a bucket collide only
with probability about $(k(k-1)/2L)^R$, self-collisions disappear for
$R\ge2$, and independent patterns decorrelate the trials.

\paragraph{Repair.}
When no pattern admits a feasible shift, \PHastR tries to make room. Using
the same words $w_1$, \dots, $w_k$, the half adder
\[
t \leftarrow t \lor (u\land w_i),\qquad u\leftarrow u\lor w_i
\]
computes in $u\land\lnot t$ the shifts at which \emph{exactly one} key is
blocked. For each such candidate, in order of increasing sum of positions, an
\emph{owner} array parallel to the cyclic occupancy bitmap gives the bucket
$Y$ occupying the blocking slot. If $Y$ is still entirely inside the active
part of the window, we evict $Y$, place the current bucket, and place $Y$
again (Figure~\ref{fig:search}(b)); if $Y$ fails, it may in turn evict another bucket (with a quarter of
the candidates), up to a maximum depth. If $Y$ cannot be placed, the eviction
is undone and the next candidate (we keep one per distinct $Y$) is tried. A
failing bucket is bumped only after $16$ unsuccessful candidates. Repair turns
the greedy sweep of \PHast into a local search, as in PtrHash~\cite{RagPTR},
but it is triggered only by failures, and bumping remains as a safety valve:
the structure stays minimal and needs no slack in the output range.

\paragraph{Slice length.}
As in \PHast, the slice length depends on the size of the level: the first
and last $L+D$ positions are reached by fewer slices than the others, so
small levels need shorter slices. We use $L=64$ when the range of the level
is smaller than $2500$, $128$ below $4000$, $256$ below $70\,000$, $512$
below $600\,000$, and a maximum, which is a parameter, above (and always
$L\le m/2$); the thresholds have been tuned experimentally for $S=8$. With
repair, longer slices than in \PHastP pay off for large levels: for $S=8$ the
best maximum is $L=1024$, whereas \PHastP uses $L=512$. \PHast uses a similar
table with different thresholds ($64$ below $1300$, $128$ below $9500$, $256$
below $12\,000$, $512$ below $140\,000$, then $1024$ for $S<12$), whereas
\PHastP uses a fixed length depending only on $S$, limited by the range of
the level. The priority weights used by the sweep depend on the slice length,
and are chosen for each level accordingly; those for $S=8$ and $L=512$ or
$L=1024$, and for $S=10$ and $L=2048$, have been retuned for \PHastR by
coordinate descent (the others are those of \PHastP).

\paragraph{Levels.}
As in \PHastP, keys of bumped buckets are hashed again at each following
level with a different seed, and the new hash $h'$ determines their bucket and
slice. Offsets are provided by $o\oplus h'$, where $o$ is the value of the
first level: a query reaching further levels has already computed $o$ (in
parallel with the access to the seed of the first level), and needs a further
hash of the key at each level, but no further multiplication. Since $h'$ is
independent of $h$, the offsets of a key at each level are independent of
those of the previous levels and of the new bucket and slice. Levels after the
first use the same scheme with range equal to their number of keys. As in
\PHastP, an Elias--Fano sequence maps the outputs of the levels after the
first to the holes of the first level.

\paragraph{Colliding signatures.}
Keys with the same $64$-bit hash $h$ at the first level have the same slice and
the same offsets, so their bucket self-collides for every seed and it is
bumped; at the following levels they are hashed again, and thus separated. As
in \PHastP, $64$-bit hashes are therefore sufficient for any number of keys,
and no larger signature is necessary. Construction would fail only if two
keys had the same hash at all levels, which happens in practice only for
duplicate keys: to report duplicates before building the levels, keys whose
hashes at the first level coincide are hashed again with a different seed,
and keys whose hashes coincide again are considered duplicates.

\paragraph{Last level.}
When at most $4096$ keys are left, a last level uses the same placement
scheme with a range larger by $25\%$ plus $16$ slots, expected bucket size $\min(\lambda,3)$, and
no bumping, hashing the keys again with a different seed (and, every eight
attempts, enlarging the range) in the unlikely case of failure. \PHastP behaves differently:
when at most $8192$ keys are left ($4096$ for the variants with wrapping), it
builds a level of regular \PHast (pseudorandom placement, all $256$ seeds of
$8$ bits tried for each bucket, expected bucket size $4$, no bumping) with
range $1.2(k+10)$, where $k$ is the number of keys, rehashing all keys with a
new seed in case of failure; queries reaching this level hash the key again.
In particular, for small sets (up to about $10^5$ keys, as \PHastP bumps
$7.6\%$ of the keys) all bumped keys go directly from the first level to a
level of regular \PHast.

\paragraph{Parallel construction.}
As in \PHast, buckets are split into chunks separated by gaps of
\[
\left\lfloor \frac{(L+D-1)B}{m-L-D+2}\right\rfloor+1
\]
buckets: the slice of bucket $b$ starts at about $b(m-L-D+2)/B$, and the
positions of a key lie at most $L+D-2$ slots after the start of its slice, so
(considering the rounding of slice starts) the positions of different chunks
cannot overlap. Chunks are swept in parallel. Gaps are then processed
differently: \PHast processes them serially, continuing for each gap the
sweep of the previous chunk (whose state, including the priority queue, is
preserved) after adding to its occupancy the positions of the first buckets
of the following chunk; \PHastR sweeps all gaps in parallel, each with a new
sweep in which the positions of the buckets of the neighboring chunks (on both
sides) are marked as occupied and \emph{not evictable}, as repair must not
move buckets owned by another chunk.

\paragraph{Summary of the differences with \PHastP.}
Besides patterns and repair, \PHastR differs from \PHastP in the following
respects: the seed encodes a pattern and a shift, and offsets are taken from a
value $o$ derived from the hash of the first level (Placement), also at
the following levels, where offsets are given by $o$ xored with the hash of
the level (Levels); the slice length adapts to the size of the level,
and it is longer for large levels (Slice length); the last level uses the
same scheme as the other levels instead of regular \PHast (Last level); and
gaps between parallel chunks are swept in parallel by independent sweeps
instead of serially by continuing the previous sweep (Parallel construction).
Bucket priorities have the same form as in \PHastP (a weight depending on
the size of the bucket, extrapolated linearly beyond size $7$, minus $1024$
times the index of the bucket), with retuned weights.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We ran all experiments on an Apple M1 Max with 64\,GiB of RAM, compiling with
Rust 1.98 at optimization level 3 with thin link-time optimization. Keys are
distinct 64-bit integers. The reference implementation is the \texttt{ph}
crate by Beling (\texttt{Function2} with the \texttt{ShiftOnly},
\texttt{ShiftOnlyWrapped<$\delta$>} and \texttt{SeedOnly} seed choosers for
\PHastP, \PHastP with wrapping and \PHast, respectively). To make query times
comparable we fed both implementations the same hash function, XXH3-64 with a
seed. Construction times are single-threaded unless otherwise noted and
include hashing; query times are the best of three averages over $10^7$
queries of random keys of the set. \PHastR uses $R=4$ patterns, $16$ repair
candidates, and byte seeds for $S=8$ and bit-packed seeds otherwise.

\section{The Limits of Repair}
\label{sec:limits}

Since repair is responsible for a large part of the improvement of \PHastR over
\PHastP, it is natural to ask how far it can go: if we let repairs proceed with
no bound on their depth or breadth, can we get rid of bumping altogether? In
this section we show that the answer is negative, for two different reasons.
The first reason is structural, and it applies to every placement scheme of the
\PHast family, with or without repair: no placement of the keys, however found,
can avoid bumping. The second reason is algorithmic: most failures of the sweep
cannot be repaired at any depth, and repairing the other ones makes the sweep
fail more often.

\subsection{A Lower Bound on Bumping}

In all structures of the \PHast family a key whose slice starts at $\sigma$ can
be placed only in the \emph{window} $[\sigma\..\sigma+W)$, where $W=L$ for
\PHast, $W=L+2^S-2$ for \PHastP, and $W=L+D-1$ for \PHastR. The seed of a
bucket chooses positions within the windows of its keys, but the windows
themselves depend only on the hashes of the keys. This simple constraint is
sufficient to force bumping.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:bump}
Consider $n$ keys and $n$ slots, and assume that, for some $W\geq 1$, each key
$x$ can be placed only in the slots $[\sigma_x\..\sigma_x+W)$. Let
\[
X(t) = t - \bigl|\{\,x \mid \sigma_x < t\,\}\bigr|\qquad (0\leq t\leq n),
\]
and let $R=\max_t X(t)-\min_t X(t)$ be the range of $X$. Then, every injective
placement of a subset of the keys leaves at least $R-W+1$ keys unplaced.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $0\leq s\leq t\leq n$ the number of keys $x$ with $s\leq\sigma_x<t$ is
$t-s-\bigl(X(t)-X(s)\bigr)$. Only these keys can occupy the $t-s-W+1$ slots
in $[s+W-1\..t)$, so at least $X(t)-X(s)-W+1$ of these slots are empty.
Symmetrically, these keys can occupy only the at most $t-s+W-1$ slots in
$[s\..t+W-1)$, so at least $X(s)-X(t)-W+1$ of them are unplaced. Since there
are as many keys as slots, the number of unplaced keys is equal to the number
of empty slots, and it is thus at least $\bigl|X(t)-X(s)\bigr|-W+1$ for all
$s\leq t$. Choosing as $s$ and $t$ the points at which $X$ attains its minimum
and its maximum, in the appropriate order, completes the proof.
\end{proof}

The proposition is a quantitative version of the (easy) necessity of Hall's
condition~\cite{HalORS} for the bipartite graph connecting keys and slots. In
the \PHast family slice starts are computed from a hash by inversion in
fixed-point arithmetic, so we can model them as independent and uniformly
distributed. If the $\sigma_x$ are independent and uniform in $[0\..n)$, then
$R=nV_n$, where $V_n$ is Kuiper's statistic~\cite{KuiTCRPC} of the values
$\sigma_x/n$, that is, the sum of the maximum positive and negative deviations
of their empirical distribution from the uniform distribution (the compression
of slice starts into $[0\..n-W+1)$ changes $R$ by at most $W-1$). By Donsker's
theorem~\cite{BilCPM} and the continuity of the range, $R/\sqrt n$ converges in
distribution to the range of a standard Brownian bridge, which by Vervaat's
transformation~\cite{VerRBBBE} has the same distribution as the maximum of a
standard Brownian excursion, whose mean is $\sqrt{\pi/2}$~\cite{KenDMBE}. Since
the Dvoretzky--Kiefer--Wolfowitz inequality~\cite{MasTCDKWI} gives $R/\sqrt n$
exponentially decreasing tails, the convergence extends to expectations, and
we conclude that
\[
\mathbf E[R]=\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}2}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).
\]
We verified the estimate experimentally: over $200$ random sets of $2^{12}$,
$2^{16}$, and $2^{20}$ keys, the average range is within $1\%$ of
$\sqrt{\pi n/2}$.

The bound is irrelevant in terms of space. For \PHastR with $S=8$ and $L=1024$
we have $W=1087$, so on $10^7$ keys at least about $2900$ keys ($0.03\%$) must
be bumped, and on $10^9$ keys about $38\,500$ ($0.004\%$), whereas, as we will
see shortly, the first level of \PHastR bumps about $2.6\%$ of the keys. Its
importance lies in the fact that it applies to \emph{every} placement: as soon
as $\sqrt{\pi n/2}$ is significantly larger than $W$, that is, beyond a few
hundred thousand keys, no amount of search can make a first level with $m=n$
free of bumping. In particular, it rules out the possibility of obtaining a
bump-free version of \PHastR, whose space would be just $S/\lambda$ bits per
key, by a backtracking search in the style of
CONSENSUS~\cite{LSWCSESAMPH}, which combines the search and the encoding of
seeds to get arbitrarily close to the lower bound for MPHFs. For such a search
to succeed, keys should be first split into parts of exactly known size $C$,
with $\sqrt{\pi C/2}$ significantly smaller than $W$, as it happens (albeit for
different reasons) in Bucketed CONSENSUS-RecSplit, whose first step is a minimal
$k$-perfect hash function.

Even within such parts, however, a second obstacle appears. Under the
minimum-sum rule, whether a slot will be occupied is decided by buckets whose
slices start up to $W$ slots before it, that is, about $W/\lambda$ buckets
before the slot becomes unreachable, so a depth-first search that detects a
failure when a slot becomes unreachable must exhaust the alternatives of all
such buckets before it can change the culprit. In a prototype using chained
seeds as in CONSENSUS (the offsets of a bucket depend on the seeds of the
previous buckets, so that backtracking rerandomizes the following ones), a
cyclic output range to avoid boundary effects, and a check that all unreachable
slots are occupied, the search never placed more than $160$ buckets, even with
more than two bits of slack per bucket and a budget of thousands of placements
per bucket.

The bump rate of \PHastR is two orders of magnitude larger than the lower bound
of Proposition~\ref{prop:bump}, so the latter is not what limits repair in
practice. Table~\ref{tab:depth} shows the fraction of keys bumped from the first
level of \PHastR as a function of the maximum depth of repair. The decrease is
geometric: depth one removes more than $80\%$ of the bumps that repair can
remove at all, and each further level removes about three quarters of the
remaining ones. The limit, about $2.42\%$ for $\lambda=4.75$ and $4.43\%$ for
$\lambda=5.25$, is $63\%$ and $73\%$, respectively, of the bump rate without
repair. Breadth saturates in the same way: at depth one and $\lambda=4.75$, the
bump rate is $3.59\%$, $3.13\%$, $2.81\%$, $2.61\%$, $2.59\%$, and $2.59\%$ with
$1$, $4$, $8$, $16$, $32$, and $64$ candidates.

Thus, a failure is either \emph{repairable}, in which case a short chain of
evictions suffices, or \emph{unrepairable}, in which case no chain of
evictions exists within the region that repair can reach: the blockers of an
unrepairable bucket are typically buckets whose windows lie mostly in the fully
occupied region behind the frontier of the sweep, and thus cannot be moved.

Letting repairs proceed with no bounds, as in PtrHash~\cite{RagPTR}, makes
things worse. In a simulator of the sweep (with the placement of \PHastR on a
cyclic output range) in which an evicted bucket evicts in turn the
cheapest blockers (with a tabu list to avoid short cycles), chains of evictions
drift backwards, as the owner of a blocking slot is usually a bucket whose slice
starts earlier than that of the evicting one. When a chain reaches a bucket
whose window is entirely occupied, every seed blocks several keys, every
eviction causes several further evictions, and the process becomes
supercritical: on $2^{16}$ keys with $\lambda=5$, about $9\%$ of the repairs
did not terminate within $10^4$ evictions, both with $m=n$ and with $5\%$ of
slack in the output range. Confining evictions to buckets near the frontier
stops the drift, but repairs then either terminate after a few evictions or
cycle forever.

Finally, repair has a feedback effect on the sweep. Since with $m=n$ holes and
bumped keys are the same thing (Section~\ref{sec:anatomy}), every repaired
failure fills a slot that would otherwise have remained empty: the region
around the frontier becomes denser, and more buckets fail at their first
placement. The reduction of bumping is thus smaller than the fraction of
failures that repair fixes: the sweep settles into a new equilibrium, which
repair, as any increase in flexibility, moves, but without removing bumping.

In practice, then, depth one or two is not a compromise: from depth two to
depth four the bump rate decreases by less than $0.1$ percentage points, and
space by less than $0.007$ bits per key, while construction time keeps
increasing.

\section{Conclusions}

The excess space of \PHastP is the cost of bumping, and a large part of it is
caused by the rigidity of additive placement. Independent offset patterns
remove the rigidity without losing bit-parallelism, and a half adder over the
same words that test feasibility turns failures into cheap, local cuckoo
repairs. The resulting structure, \PHastR, has the query of \PHastP, the space
of \PHast, and an intermediate construction time. Repair has, however, intrinsic
limits: no placement in which keys move within windows of $W$ slots can avoid
bumping about $\sqrt{\pi n/2}-W$ keys, and in practice most failures of the
sweep cannot be repaired at any depth, so shallow repairs capture almost all of
the benefit.

There are several open problems and further research directions. On the
practical side, the bucket priority should be retuned for larger seeds, access
to bit-packed seeds should be made faster, and the last levels could be replaced
by a more compact structure. More interestingly, the lower bound of
Section~\ref{sec:limits} does not apply to keys split into parts of exactly
known size: whether bump-free placements, which would use just $S/\lambda$ bits
per key, can be found efficiently within such parts, in spite of the long-range
constraints that defeat backtracking, is an open problem.

The implementation is available in the \texttt{sux} crate as
\texttt{sux::func::PHastR}.
