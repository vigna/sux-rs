\begin{abstract}
We present \PHastR, a minimal perfect hash function of the \PHast family. Like
its fast-to-build variant \PHastP, \PHastR finds feasible seeds with
bit-parallel operations, as its seeds move keys along \emph{rings} of slots
that are scanned by rotating a word per key; but it avoids
\emph{self-collisions}, pairs of keys that collide for (almost) all seeds of
their bucket, which account for almost $40\%$ of the keys bumped by \PHastP
with wrapping. With 8-bit seeds, \PHastR uses $1.93$ bits per key against the
$1.97$ of \PHastP with wrapping, it is built in half the time, and its queries
are faster; its space and its query time are close to those of \PHast, which
takes more than ten times as long to build. With 10-bit seeds it uses $1.85$
bits per key against $1.87$. Finally, we prove that when keys can move only
within windows of $W$ slots no placement of $n$ keys on $n$ slots, however
found, can avoid bumping about $\sqrt{\pi n/2}-W$ keys.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A \emph{minimal perfect hash function} (MPHF) maps a static set of $n$ keys
bijectively to $[0\..n)$; it requires at least $\lg e\approx 1.443$
bits per key (we use $\lg$ for the binary logarithm and $\ln$ for the natural
logarithm). Among the many practical constructions, \emph{bucket placement}
gives the fastest queries: keys are hashed to buckets, and a per-bucket seed
chooses positions for the keys of the bucket so that no two keys collide.

\PHast~\cite{BeSPHast} pushes this approach to its limit: the seeds are
stored in a plain array of fixed-width values, each seed places the keys of
its bucket inside small overlapping slices of the output range, and a query
reads a single seed for almost all keys. Finding the seeds, however, is
expensive, as \PHast tries all the seeds of each bucket: construction takes
hundreds of nanoseconds per key. Its variant \PHastP finds feasible seeds
with bit-parallel operations, and it is an order of magnitude faster to
build, but with 8-bit seeds it needs $2.12$ bits per key instead of $1.92$,
or $1.97$ in its variants with wrapping, which are slower to build.

In this paper we present \PHastR, a structure of the same family with the
space and the query time of \PHast and a construction faster than that of
\PHastP. This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \PHastR, a structure in which the lowest bits of the seed choose one of
$R$ independent patterns, obtained at no cost from the computation of the
bucket, and the seeds of a pattern move a key through a ring of slots; rings
make it possible to find all feasible seeds of a pattern by rotating blocks of
bits (Section~\ref{sec:phastr});
\item an analysis of the differences with \PHast and \PHastP, showing that
the excess space of \PHastP, with and without wrapping, comes from bumping,
that a large part of the bumping is caused by self-collisions inherent to
additive placement, and that patterns make them disappear
(Section~\ref{sec:differences});
\item an implementation in the Rust crate \texttt{sux}, an experimental
comparison with the reference implementation of \PHast and \PHastP
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), and an offline construction that reads the
keys just once, needs less than a byte per key of memory, and builds the same
structure (Section~\ref{sec:offline});
\item a lower bound on the number of bumped keys that holds for every placement
in which keys move within windows of fixed length, which implies that on large
key sets no search can produce a first level free of bumping
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{itemize}

\section{\PHastR}
\label{sec:phastr}

\PHastR follows the scheme of \PHast~\cite{BeSPHast}; we discuss the
differences in Section~\ref{sec:differences}. Each key has a $64$-bit hash,
which determines its \emph{bucket}, among $B$ buckets, and its \emph{slice},
a range of $L$ consecutive slots of the output range $[0\..m)$ whose start
grows linearly with the hash. Each bucket has a seed, stored in a plain array
of $S$-bit values, which places the keys of the bucket in their slices; a
seed is \emph{feasible} for a bucket if it maps its keys to distinct free
slots. Buckets for which no seed is feasible are \emph{bumped} (seed~$0$),
and their keys are handled by further levels. The first level has output
range $m=n$, so every bumped key leaves a free slot (a \emph{hole}) in the
first level, and an Elias--Fano sequence~\cite{EliESRCASF,FanNBRISM} maps the
outputs of the further levels to the holes. A query thus reads a single seed
for almost all keys. Seeds are assigned by a sweep over the buckets that keeps
a small window of buckets in a priority queue, favoring large buckets, and
chooses for each bucket the feasible seed that minimizes the sum of the
positions of its keys.

Each hole costs about $2+\lg(1/\beta)$ bits in the Elias--Fano remapping,
where $\beta$ is the fraction of bumped keys, and each bumped key must be
placed again by the following levels. With $S$-bit seeds and expected bucket
size $\lambda=n/B$ the space is thus approximately
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:space}
\frac{S}{\lambda(1-\beta)} + \beta\Bigl(2+\lg\frac1\beta\Bigr),
\end{equation}
and since the entropy of a random set of $\beta n$ holes is already about
$\beta n(\lg(1/\beta)+\lg e)$, the encoding of holes cannot be
substantially improved: once $S$ and $\lambda$ are fixed, \emph{the only
lever is $\beta$}. We will show in Section~\ref{sec:limits} that on large key
sets no placement, however found, can avoid bumping altogether; the purpose of
the design of \PHastR is to bump few keys while finding feasible seeds with
bit-parallel operations.

\paragraph{Placement.}
Let $h$ be the hash of a key: its $64$ bits fill a machine word. We require
the number $B$ of buckets to be odd,
and denote with $\hi(x)$ and $\operatorname{lo}(x)$ the upper and lower $64$
bits of a $128$-bit product. The bucket of a key is $\hi(hB)$, and the start
of its slice is $\sigma(h)=\hi(h(m-L+1))$. Let moreover $R=2^\rho$ be the
number of \emph{patterns}, and $L=2^\ell$ the slice length, with $R\ell\le 64$
and, for the time being, $\ell\geq S$. We let $o=\operatorname{lo}(hB)$: the
$r$-th pattern assigns to the key the in-slice offset
\[
\omega_r(o)=\bigl\lfloor o/2^{64r/R}\bigr\rfloor \bmod L,
\]
that is, the $\ell$ bits of $o$ starting at bit $64r/R$. A seed
$s\in[1\..2^S)$ selects the pattern $r=s\bmod R$, and the key is mapped to
\[
\sigma(h)+\bigl(\omega_r(o)+s\cdot 2^{\ell-S}\bigr)\bmod L.
\]

The seeds of pattern $r$ are $r$, $r+R$, $r+2R$,~\dots, so they move a key by
multiples of the \emph{stride} $T=RL/2^S$: as the seed varies, the key goes
exactly once through the $2^S/R=L/T$ slots of its slice that are congruent to
$\sigma(h)+\omega_r(o)+r2^{\ell-S}$ modulo $T$. We call these slots a
\emph{ring} (Figure~\ref{fig:placement}). In other words, the seed first
chooses among $R$ patterns, and then moves the keys along the rings of the
chosen one. Two keys of a bucket collide for all seeds of a pattern if they
are mapped to the same slot by the first one (a \emph{self-collision}), but
since the offsets of different patterns are disjoint blocks of bits of $o$,
this happens independently in each pattern: a bucket of size $k$ has no usable
pattern with probability about $(k(k-1)/2L)^R$, which is negligible already
for $R=2$. Moreover, different patterns provide (almost) independent trials.

The value $o$ costs nothing, as it is the other half of the product that
yields the bucket, and it is a good source of offsets: for the keys of bucket
$b$ we have $hB=b\cdot2^{64}+o$, so $o$ is the position of $h$ in the interval
of hashes of the bucket, scaled to $64$ bits, and it is uniformly distributed
if $h$ is. (Since $B$ is odd, also the lowest bits of $o$ depend bijectively
on those of $h$.) In particular, the offsets of the keys of a bucket are
independent of each other for every pattern, which would not be true of
blocks of bits of $h$, as the keys of a bucket share the upper
${\approx}\lg B$ bits of $h$.

\paragraph{Queries.}
A query computes $hB$, reads the seed $s$ of bucket $\hi(hB)$, and, unless
$s=0$, computes the formula above. There is no need to extract $r$: since
$(s\cdot 64/R)\bmod 64=r\cdot 64/R$, the offset $\omega_r(o)$ is obtained by
shifting $o$ right by $s\cdot 64/R$ modulo $64$, and the lower bits of the
result are extracted by the reduction modulo $L$ that follows the addition of
$s\cdot2^{\ell-S}$. Since $o$ fills a word, the reduction modulo $64$ costs
nothing: processors perform it on shift amounts anyway.

\paragraph{Search.}
The positions of a key for the seeds of a pattern are $T$ slots apart, but
since $T$ is a power of two and it divides $L$ we can make them adjacent: we
store the set of used slots by residue classes modulo $T$, that is, as $T$
\emph{rows} in which bit $q$ of row $\varrho$ is associated with slot
$qT+\varrho$. The ring of a key is then a block of $L/T$ consecutive bits of a
row, and the seed of index $j$ of a pattern (i.e., $jR+r$) maps the key to the
bit of its ring of index $(x+j)\bmod (L/T)$, where $x$ is the index for the
first seed. Thus, the bits telling which seeds of a pattern map a key to a
used slot are the bits of its ring rotated by $x$, and combining them for all
the keys of the bucket by a bitwise \textsc{or} we obtain a block whose zeros
are the feasible seeds (Figure~\ref{fig:search}). For $S=8$ and $R=4$ a ring
has $2^S/R=64$ slots whatever the slice length, so it fills a word: each
pattern requires just reading and rotating a word for each key. Note that in
the first pattern the first seed is $0$, which is not available.

Among feasible seeds we choose, as in \PHast, the one minimizing the sum of
the slots of the keys. For a bucket of size $k$, the sum for the seed of index
$j$ of a pattern is the sum for the first seed of the pattern plus $kjT$,
minus $L$ for each key that has gone past the end of its ring, that is, for
each key such that $x+j\geq L/T$. To count such keys we pack the indices $x$ of
the keys of the bucket into fields of a word that are large enough to contain
the sum of two indices (for $S=8$ and $R=4$, eight fields of eight bits):
after adding $j$ to all fields with a single addition, the bit of weight $L/T$
of each field tells whether the corresponding key has gone past the end of its
ring, and counting such bits gives the number of keys. Evaluating a seed thus
requires a number of operations that does not depend on the size of the
bucket (for buckets with at most eight keys, in the case above), and since
buckets have usually just a few feasible seeds (on average $4.5$ with
$\lambda=5$) we
evaluate all of them, with a single loop over the zeros of the blocks of all
patterns that contains no other branch. When there are many feasible seeds
(e.g., at the beginning of a sweep) we use instead the fact that in a pattern
the sum increases with the index of the seed, except at the indices at which
some key goes back to the first slot of its ring: thus, we need to consider
only the first feasible index and, for each key, the first feasible index
after the key went back, that is, at most $k+1$ candidates per pattern.
Finally, when marking the slots of the best candidate as used we check that
no two keys of the bucket are mapped to the same slot, and otherwise we move
to the next candidate.

\paragraph{Grouping and scheduling.}
The sweep needs the hashes of the keys of each bucket, but in no particular
order, and we do not want to keep more than one copy of them. Thus, we
distribute them into at most $256$ \emph{parts} made of a power of two of
consecutive buckets: each thread computes the hashes of a chunk of the keys,
and writes each of them at the end of the \emph{region} of its part, in the
order of the keys. Regions are sized before computing the hashes, assuming
that hashes are uniform, with a slack of six standard deviations: the few
hashes that do not fit their region, if any, are kept separately. A part is
distributed into buckets only when a sweep needs it, into a buffer of the
sweep (the part is distributed first into smaller parts that fit the cache,
and then each smaller part into its buckets), so regions are never modified:
after the sweep, the keys of bumped buckets, which must be passed to the next
level, are found by walking the keys of each chunk in order with a byte per
key recording its part, and reading their hashes from the regions. Writing to
at most $256$ regions at a time and working on parts of about $150$\,KB is
efficient on any current architecture, and the construction needs, besides
the hashes, just a byte per key: about $11$ bytes per key overall.

The priority of a bucket depends on its size and decreases with its index,
and buckets enter the window of the sweep in order of index: thus, the
buckets of each size in the window form a queue, and the bucket with the
highest priority is the first bucket of one of the queues. Instead of a
priority queue we keep a queue for each size: extracting the next bucket
requires scanning the first elements of a few queues, and adding a bucket
takes constant time. Finally, the set of used slots of the level, which is
necessary to build the following levels, is a byproduct of the sweep: the
bits of the slots that are no longer reachable are copied to the bitmap of
the level when their part of the set of used slots is recycled.

\paragraph{Slice length.}
The slice length of a level with output range $m$ is the smallest power of two
larger than $m/2$, up to a maximum, which is a parameter: we use $L=1024$ for
$S=8$ and $L=2048$ for $S=10$, as \PHastP with wrapping does. The slices of
small levels can thus be shorter than the number of seeds, that is, $\ell<S$:
in this case seed $s$ moves a key by $s$, so the stride is $R$, rings have
$L/R$ slots (a single slot if $L<R$), and different seeds of a pattern map
the keys of a bucket to the same slots. The priority of a bucket has the same
form as in \PHast (a weight depending on the size of the bucket, extrapolated
linearly beyond size $7$, minus $1024$ times the index of the bucket), and we
use the weights of \PHastP with wrapping.

\paragraph{Levels.}
Levels after the first use the same scheme with range equal to their number of
keys, and an Elias--Fano sequence maps their outputs to the holes of the first
level. A bumped key is hashed again just once, with a different seed: if $h'$
is this second hash, the value playing the role of $h$ at level $i>1$ is
\[
\hi\bigl((h\oplus z_i)(h'\oplus c)\bigr)\oplus\operatorname{lo}\bigl((h\oplus z_i)(h'\oplus c)\bigr),
\]
where $z_i$ is a \emph{salt} associated with the level and $c$ is a constant.
Thus, after a bumped bucket is detected and the second hash is computed the
key is no longer necessary: the levels after the first are accessed by code
that depends just on two integers, which can be kept separate from the code
accessing the first level without the need of preserving the key.

\paragraph{Colliding signatures.}
Keys with the same $64$-bit hash at the first level have the same slice and
the same offsets in all patterns, so they collide for every seed and their
bucket is bumped; at the following levels their values depend also on the
second hash, and thus they are separated. Since about $n^2/2^{65}$ pairs of
keys have the same hash (about three for $n=10^{10}$), the keys bumped in this
way are negligible with respect to the $\beta n$ keys that are bumped anyway.
Hence, $64$-bit hashes are
sufficient for any number of keys, and no larger signature is
necessary. Construction would fail only if two keys had the same pair of
hashes, which happens in practice only for duplicate keys. Since duplicate
keys are always bumped from the first level, to report them we examine the
buckets of the following levels: keys of a bucket with the same value are
hashed with a third seed, and keys whose hashes coincide again are considered
duplicates.

\paragraph{Last level.}
When at most $4096$ keys are left, a last level uses the same placement
scheme with a range larger by $25\%$ plus $16$ slots, expected bucket size $\min(\lambda,3)$, and
no bumping, changing the salt (and, every eight
attempts, enlarging the range) in the unlikely case of failure.

\paragraph{Parallel construction.}
As in \PHast, buckets are split into chunks separated by gaps of
\[
\left\lfloor \frac{LB}{m-L+1}\right\rfloor+1
\]
buckets: the slice of bucket $b$ starts at about $b(m-L+1)/B$, and the
positions of a key lie at most $L-1$ slots after the start of its slice, so
(considering the rounding of slice starts) the positions of different chunks
cannot overlap. Chunks are swept in parallel, and then all gaps are swept in
parallel, each with a new sweep in which the positions of the buckets of the
neighboring chunks (on both sides) are marked as used.

\section{Differences from \PHast and \PHastP}
\label{sec:differences}

In \PHast~\cite{BeSPHast}, in its variant \PHastP, and in \PHastR the bucket
of a key with hash $h$ is $\hi(hB)$, and the seed $s$ of the bucket places the
key at $\sigma(h)+p(s,h)$, where $\sigma(h)=\hi(h(m-L+1))$ is the start of its
slice: the three structures differ mainly in the in-slice position $p(s,h)$.
In \PHast, $p(s,h)$ is a pseudorandom function, so finding the best seed means
trying all $2^S-1$ of them; construction takes hundreds of nanoseconds per
key. \PHastP uses instead the additive placement $p(s,h)=(h \bmod L)+s-1$:
the feasibility of $64$ consecutive seeds of a bucket is the complement of the
bitwise \textsc{or} of $64$-bit windows of the occupancy bitmap, one per key,
and the best seed is the first feasible one. Construction becomes an order of
magnitude faster, but with 8-bit seeds space grows from $1.92$ to $2.12$ bits
per key. \PHastP \emph{with wrapping} uses $p(s,h)=(h+\delta s)\bmod L$ for a
small multiplier $\delta$, so that keys do not leave their slice, and reduces
space to $1.97$ bits per key ($\delta=3$) at the cost of a slower
construction. In \PHastR, $p(s,h)=\bigl(\omega_r(o)+s\cdot 2^{\ell-S}\bigr)\bmod
L$, where $r=s\bmod R$ and $o=\operatorname{lo}(hB)$.

The measurements of the next two paragraphs refer to $10^7$ random keys and
8-bit seeds, with the space-optimal configurations of \PHastP (expected bucket
size $\lambda=5.25$ and slice length $L=512$) and of \PHastP with wrapping
($\delta=3$, $\lambda=5$, $L=1024$). They have been obtained on the
reference implementation (the \texttt{ph} crate, using GxHash as
hash function), whose first level we inspect through read-only accessors.

\paragraph{Holes are bumped keys.}
As in \PHastR, the first level of \PHastP has output range $m=n$, so its holes
are exactly its bumped keys, and its space is approximately given
by~(\ref{eq:space}). Without wrapping $\beta=7.6\%$, and bumping costs $0.59$
of the $2.12$ bits per key ($0.47$ for Elias--Fano, $0.13$ for the further
levels); with wrapping $\beta=4.3\%$, and bumping costs $0.37$ of the $1.97$
bits per key ($0.30$ and $0.07$).

\paragraph{Additive placement is rigid.}
Additive placement translates a fixed pattern: two keys of a bucket whose base
positions coincide collide for every seed. For a bucket of size $k$ this
happens with probability about $k(k-1)/(2L)$, so without wrapping a fraction
$\lambda^2/(2L)\approx 2.7\%$ of the buckets cannot be placed at all
(we measure $2.6\%$); they are $38.5\%$ of the bumped buckets and contain
about $(\lambda^2+2\lambda)/(2L)\approx 3.7\%$ of the keys (we measure
$3.6\%$). Wrapping does not change the picture: two keys with the same base
position are moved together around their slices, and collide for all seeds
but the few for which exactly one of them has wrapped. The slices of the
variant with wrapping are longer, so these \emph{self-collisions} are half as
frequent, but so is bumping: they happen in $1.2\%$ of the buckets, which
contain $1.7\%$ of the keys, that is, $39\%$ of the bumped keys. Moreover,
consecutive seeds are strongly correlated trials. Seeds, on the other hand,
are almost incompressible: their empirical entropy is $7.71$ bits without
wrapping and $7.89$ bits with wrapping.

\paragraph{Patterns instead of a rigid pattern.}
In \PHastP, with or without wrapping, all seeds move the same rigid pattern of
base positions. In \PHastR the seed first chooses among $R$ patterns, whose
offsets are taken from disjoint blocks of bits of the lower half of the
product yielding the bucket, rather than from the hash, and then moves the
keys along the rings of the chosen pattern, using as multiplier the power of
two $L/2^S$ rather than a small odd number. Since self-collisions happen
independently in each pattern (Section~\ref{sec:phastr}), they disappear
already for $R=2$. In Figure~\ref{fig:placement}, for example, the keys $x_1$
and $x_2$ would collide for all seeds in \PHastP, whereas in \PHastR they
collide just for the seeds of pattern~$0$. Moreover, different patterns
provide (almost) independent trials. With $R=1$ and $\delta=2^{\ell-S}$,
\PHastR is essentially \PHastP with wrapping (which, however, uses odd
multipliers).

\paragraph{Queries and search.}
After reading the seed, \PHastP with wrapping multiplies it by $\delta$, adds
the hash, reduces the result modulo $L$, and adds $\sigma(h)$; \PHastR
performs the same operations, with a shift in place of the multiplication and
$o$ in place of the hash, after computing $s\cdot 64/R$ and shifting $o$: two
shifts more. In \PHastP the positions of a key for consecutive seeds are
consecutive slots, so the seeds that are feasible for a bucket are found by
combining windows of the bitmap of used slots; with wrapping, however, the
range of seeds must be split at the points at which some key wraps, and with
$\delta>1$ only one bit out of $\delta$ of each window is meaningful. In
\PHastR the positions of a key for the seeds of a pattern are adjacent bits of
a row of the set of used slots, and the wrapping of a ring is taken care of by
a rotation.

\paragraph{Construction.}
The construction of \PHastR differs from that of the reference implementation
of \PHast and \PHastP also in several engineering aspects
(Section~\ref{sec:phastr}). The reference implementation sorts the hashes in
place, and hashes all keys again to find those of bumped buckets; \PHastR
distributes the hashes into parts, which are distributed into buckets only
when a sweep needs them, and finds the keys of bumped buckets by walking the
keys, using about $11$ bytes per key against $10$. The priority queue of the
sweep is replaced by a queue for each bucket size, and the set of used slots
of a level is a byproduct of the sweep. In \PHastP the keys of bumped buckets
are hashed again at each following level with a different seed, and the new
hash determines their bucket, their slice, and their offsets; in \PHastR they
are hashed again just once. When at most $8192$ keys are left ($4096$ for the
variants with wrapping), \PHastP builds a level of regular \PHast
(pseudorandom placement, all $256$ seeds of $8$ bits tried for each bucket,
expected bucket size $4$, no bumping) with range $1.2(k+10)$, where $k$ is the
number of keys, rehashing all keys with a new seed in case of failure; the last
level of \PHastR uses instead the same scheme as the other levels. Finally,
\PHast processes the gaps between the chunks of a parallel construction
serially, continuing for each gap the sweep of the previous chunk (whose
state, including the priority queue, is preserved) after adding to its
occupancy the positions of the first buckets of the following chunk, whereas
\PHastR sweeps all gaps in parallel. Slice lengths and bucket priorities are
those of \PHastP with wrapping; the suggested expected bucket size for 8-bit
seeds is $4.5$ instead of $5$.

\section{Offline Construction}
\label{sec:offline}

The construction of Section~\ref{sec:phastr} keeps in memory the hashes of all
keys and, as bumped keys are hashed again, the keys themselves: about $11$
bytes per key besides the keys. To build structures on hundreds of billions
of keys, \PHastR has also an offline construction, which reads the keys just
once, keeps their hashes on disk, needs in memory less than a byte per key,
and builds exactly the same structure.

\paragraph{Records.}
The value playing the role of the hash at every level depends only on the
hash~$h$ of a key and on its second hash~$h'$ (Section~\ref{sec:phastr}).
Thus, while reading the keys we compute both hashes of every key, and write a
$16$-byte \emph{record} $(h,h')$ to a temporary file: the keys are no longer
necessary, and they can be streamed, for example, from a file. The records of
a level are distributed into $P$ \emph{partitions} by the upper $\lg P$ bits
of the value playing the role of~$h$ at the level. Each partition is a
sequence of blocks of $1024$ records: the blocks of all partitions are
appended to the same file as their buffers fill, and an index with an integer
per block locates the blocks of each partition. For the first level, whose
number of keys is not known in advance, $P=2^{14}$: the buffers take
$256$\,MiB, and partitions contain a few million keys even on $10^{11}$ keys.
At the following levels, whose number~$k$ of keys is known, $P$ is the
smallest power of two not smaller than $\sqrt{2k/1024}$, which balances the
memory used by each thread writing the file, which buffers a block for each
partition, and that used by each thread sweeping the level, which buffers a
few partitions.

\paragraph{Parts.}
Since $\hi(hB)$ is a nondecreasing function of $h$, a partition contains the
keys of an interval of buckets, except that the bucket of the first value of
a partition can contain also keys of the previous partition. Offline, the
parts that sweeps load in increasing order (Section~\ref{sec:phastr}) are
made of consecutive partitions containing at least $512$ buckets, and a
bucket whose keys lie in two parts belongs to the second one. When a sweep
loads a part, it passes to the following part the keys of its first bucket
found in the part: thus, each partition is read once by each sweep, except
at the beginning of a sweep, where the previous part might need to be read
too.

\paragraph{Same structure.}
The seed chosen for a bucket depends on the set of the hashes of its keys,
but not on their order: feasible seeds are computed by a bitwise
\textsc{or}, the sum of the slots does not depend on the order of the keys,
and ties are broken by the index of the seed. Since the sweeps see the same
buckets in the same order, the structure built offline is identical to that
built in memory, provided that the number of threads is the same (it
determines the chunks of a parallel construction). The only difference is in
the detection of duplicate keys (Section~\ref{sec:phastr}): as the keys are no
longer available for a third hash, offline we consider duplicates two keys
with the same record. The two criteria differ only in the presence of two
distinct keys with the same $128$ bits of hashes, in which case both
constructions fail.

\paragraph{Following levels and memory.}
After the sweep of a level, its file is read again, and the records of the
keys of buckets without a seed are written to the file of the following
level, or kept in memory if they are at most $4096$: the file of the first
level is written once and read twice, and the following files are much
smaller. In memory, besides buffers whose size does not depend on the number
of keys, the sweep of the first level needs its seeds, as $16$-bit values,
its set of used slots, and, while merging the sets produced by the chunks of a
parallel sweep, a second copy of it: with $S=8$ and $\lambda=4.5$, about
$2/4.5+2/8\approx0.69$ bytes per key. Then, the seeds of the first level are
stored in their final format, its free slots are kept in an Elias--Fano
sequence, and the remapping sequence is built at the end from the sets of
used slots of the following levels, which are small: from this point on,
memory is essentially that of the structure being built.

\section{The Limits of the Approach}
\label{sec:limits}

The first level of \PHastR still bumps a few percent of the keys, and bumping
is where space goes: it is natural to ask whether a more flexible placement, or
a more powerful search, could get rid of it. In this section we show that the
answer is negative, for a structural reason that applies to every placement
scheme of the \PHast family: on large key sets, no placement of the keys,
however found, can avoid bumping.

In all structures of the \PHast family a key whose slice starts at $\sigma$ can
be placed only in the \emph{window} $[\sigma\..\sigma+W)$, where $W=L$ for
\PHast, for \PHastP with wrapping, and for \PHastR, and $W=L+2^S-2$ for \PHastP
without wrapping. The seed of a
bucket chooses positions within the windows of its keys, but the windows
themselves depend only on the hashes of the keys. This simple constraint is
sufficient to force bumping.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:bump}
Consider $n$ keys and $n$ slots, and assume that, for some $W\geq 1$, each key
$x$ can be placed only in the slots $[\sigma_x\..\sigma_x+W)$. Let
\[
X(t) = t - \bigl|\{\,x \mid \sigma_x < t\,\}\bigr|\qquad (0\leq t\leq n),
\]
and let $R=\max_t X(t)-\min_t X(t)$ be the range of $X$. Then, every injective
placement of a subset of the keys leaves at least $R-W+1$ keys unplaced.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $0\leq s\leq t\leq n$ the number of keys $x$ with $s\leq\sigma_x<t$ is
$t-s-\bigl(X(t)-X(s)\bigr)$. Only these keys can occupy the $t-s-W+1$ slots
in $[s+W-1\..t)$, so at least $X(t)-X(s)-W+1$ of these slots are empty.
Symmetrically, these keys can occupy only the at most $t-s+W-1$ slots in
$[s\..t+W-1)$, so at least $X(s)-X(t)-W+1$ of them are unplaced. Since there
are as many keys as slots, the number of unplaced keys is equal to the number
of empty slots, and it is thus at least $\bigl|X(t)-X(s)\bigr|-W+1$ for all
$s\leq t$. Choosing as $s$ and $t$ the points at which $X$ attains its minimum
and its maximum, in the appropriate order, completes the proof.
\end{proof}

The proposition is a quantitative version of the (easy) necessity of Hall's
condition~\cite{HalORS} for the bipartite graph connecting keys and slots. In
the \PHast family slice starts are computed from a hash by inversion in
fixed-point arithmetic, so we can model them as independent and uniformly
distributed. If the $\sigma_x$ are independent and uniform in $[0\..n)$, then
$R=nV_n$, where $V_n$ is Kuiper's statistic~\cite{KuiTCRPC} of the values
$\sigma_x/n$, that is, the sum of the maximum positive and negative deviations
of their empirical distribution from the uniform distribution (the compression
of slice starts into $[0\..n-W+1)$ changes $R$ by at most $W-1$). By Donsker's
theorem~\cite{BilCPM} and the continuity of the range, $R/\sqrt n$ converges in
distribution to the range of a standard Brownian bridge, which by Vervaat's
transformation~\cite{VerRBBBE} has the same distribution as the maximum of a
standard Brownian excursion, whose mean is $\sqrt{\pi/2}$~\cite{KenDMBE}. Since
the Dvoretzky--Kiefer--Wolfowitz inequality~\cite{MasTCDKWI} gives $R/\sqrt n$
exponentially decreasing tails, the convergence extends to expectations, and
we conclude that
\[
\mathbf E[R]=\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}2}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).
\]
We verified the estimate experimentally: over $200$ random sets of $2^{12}$,
$2^{16}$, and $2^{20}$ keys, the average range is within $1\%$ of
$\sqrt{\pi n/2}$.

The bound is irrelevant in terms of space. For \PHastR with $S=8$ and $L=1024$
we have $W=1024$, so on $10^7$ keys at least about $2900$ keys ($0.03\%$) must
be bumped, and on $10^9$ keys about $38\,600$ ($0.004\%$), whereas the first
level of \PHastR bumps a few percent of the keys. Its
importance lies in the fact that it applies to \emph{every} placement: as soon
as $\sqrt{\pi n/2}$ is significantly larger than $W$, that is, beyond a few
hundred thousand keys, no amount of search can make a first level with $m=n$
free of bumping. In particular, it rules out the possibility of obtaining a
bump-free version of \PHastR, whose space would be just $S/\lambda$ bits per
key, by a backtracking search in the style of
CONSENSUS~\cite{LSWCSESAMPH}, which combines the search and the encoding of
seeds to get arbitrarily close to the lower bound for MPHFs. For such a search
to succeed, keys should be first split into parts of exactly known size $C$,
with $\sqrt{\pi C/2}$ significantly smaller than $W$, as it happens (albeit for
different reasons) in Bucketed CONSENSUS-RecSplit, whose first step is a minimal
$k$-perfect hash function.

Even within such parts, however, a second obstacle appears. Under the
minimum-sum rule, whether a slot will be occupied is decided by buckets whose
slices start up to $W$ slots before it, that is, about $W/\lambda$ buckets
before the slot becomes unreachable, so a depth-first search that detects a
failure when a slot becomes unreachable must exhaust the alternatives of all
such buckets before it can change the culprit. In a prototype using chained
seeds as in CONSENSUS (the offsets of a bucket depend on the seeds of the
previous buckets, so that backtracking rerandomizes the following ones), a
cyclic output range to avoid boundary effects, and a check that all unreachable
slots are occupied, the search never placed more than $160$ buckets, even with
more than two bits of slack per bucket and a budget of thousands of placements
per bucket.

\section{Conclusions}

In \PHastR the lowest bits of the seed select one of a few independent
patterns, whose offsets come for free with the computation of the bucket, and
moving the keys along rings preserves bit-parallelism, as feasible seeds are
found by rotating blocks of bits of a set of used slots stored by residue
classes. Patterns remove, at the cost of two shifts per query, the rigidity
of the additive placement of \PHastP, which, even with wrapping, causes a
large part of its bumping, and thus of its excess space. As a result, \PHastR
uses less space than \PHastP with wrapping, is built in half the time, and
has faster queries; its space and its query time are close to those of
\PHast, which takes more than ten times as long to build. Bumping has, however,
intrinsic limits: no placement in which keys move within windows of $W$ slots
can avoid bumping about $\sqrt{\pi n/2}-W$ keys. Finally, an offline
construction builds the same structure keeping the hashes of the keys on disk,
with less than a byte per key of memory.

There are several open problems and further research directions. On the
practical side, the bucket priorities and the geometry of small levels should
be tuned for rings, access to bit-packed seeds should be made faster, and the
last levels could be replaced by a more compact structure. More interestingly,
the lower bound of Section~\ref{sec:limits} does not apply to keys split into
parts of exactly known size: whether bump-free placements, which would use
just $S/\lambda$ bits per key, can be found efficiently within such parts, in
spite of the long-range constraints that defeat backtracking, is an open
problem.

The implementation is available in the \texttt{sux} crate as
\texttt{sux::func::PHastR}.
